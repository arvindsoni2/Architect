The operating system

Pareto selection × Feynman compression

The goal is not to finish books. The goal is to form accurate mental models, retrieve them under pressure, and apply them to design or debugging. Every study block should produce an explanation, artefact or interview answer.

1

Frame the question

Start with an interview prompt. List what a strong answer must decide, measure and trade off.

2

Consume the 20%

Use the recommended slice. Stop when you can solve the original prompt—not when the chapter ends.

3

Explain from memory

Close the source. Teach it in plain language, draw it, then expose every vague word and missing step.

4

Prove transfer

Implement a tiny example, compare alternatives, answer aloud and revisit after 1, 3 and 7 days.

Stop rule: per topic, complete one Anchor resource, one Practice artefact and the three interview prompts before opening a Deep dive.

  3. What makes an object hashable, and why can mutable keys break dictionaries?

Fine-tuning, Transfer Learning & Post-training

Know when to adapt model weights, when to retrieve context, and when prompting is enough.

Interview 80/20

  • Prompting vs RAG vs fine-tuning: choose the cheapest mechanism that changes the right failure mode
  • SFT, LoRA, QLoRA, DPO, RLHF/GRPO and knowledge distillation at interview depth
  • Teacher → student transfer, quantisation vs distillation, and quality/cost/latency trade-offs
  • Dataset quality, eval gates, overfitting, catastrophic forgetting and deployment cost

Feynman drill

Explain to a product manager why wrong factual answers usually need retrieval, not fine-tuning. Then compare full fine-tuning, LoRA/QLoRA, knowledge distillation and quantisation to an engineer.

  2. How would you prepare a fine-tuning dataset and prevent memorisation or regressions?
  3. What does LoRA change compared with full fine-tuning, and why does QLoRA reduce memory pressure?
  4. Distillation and quantisation can both reduce serving cost. How do they differ?
