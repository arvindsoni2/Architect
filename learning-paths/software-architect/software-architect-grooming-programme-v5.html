Learn architecture through decisions, experiments and review.

Ten capability units connect business outcomes to software structure, data, operations and communication. Complete the work at a pace that allows you to test assumptions and revise decisions.

Guided practice and worked examples
How the resources fit

A learning programme with explicit limits

The curriculum guide defines capabilities. This programme provides scenarios, practice and assessment. The practice guide supplies complete examples, selected preparation and a runnable local lab. Download the learning-path folder to use the lab alongside this HTML.

This is a foundation for enterprise software and solution architecture. Passing records reviewed learning evidence; it does not certify job readiness. Responsibility in real delivery requires supervised implementation and feedback over time.

Learning loop

Predict, build evidence, explain, revise

  1. State the business rule and predict the outcome before running an example.
  2. Compare at least two credible options; reject hard-constraint violations before scoring preferences.
  3. Execute a small experiment that could disprove the recommendation.
  4. Explain the decision without jargon, invite a reviewer challenge, and revise the artefact.

Use each unit's acceptance checklist for daily review. Use the full rubric only when the capstone supplies evidence across all dimensions.

Pacing

Capability units, not a four-week readiness promise

The following are planning ranges, not performance standards. A unit may span several sessions. Experienced learners may demonstrate a bridge immediately; a learner building the foundations may need more time than these ranges.

StagePreparationGuided or independent workReview and revision
Each required bridge1–2 hours2–4 hours1–2 hours
Each core unit45–90 minutes of selected preparation30–60 minutes discussion + 2–4 hours exercise30–60 minutes, including oral defence
Capstone3–5 hours discovery15–25 hours design and experiments6–10 hours review and revision

Plan around available hours and demonstrated gaps. A 2.5-hour facilitated session can introduce a unit; it does not include all independent work. The five capstone stages are review checkpoints, not a five-day deadline.

Your workspace

Save and move your work

Export includes your notes, diagnostic decisions, scores and a visible export timestamp. Backups support up to 25 MB, including multibyte text and older long notes. Oversized exports report an error without changing notes. Keep backups private; do not commit learner records to the public repository. Existing daily notes are retained, while new diagnostics and assessment begin incomplete.

Progress measures required bridges, daily checklists, capstone evidence and assessment. It is a record of entered completion, not an independent verification of competence.

Entry pathways

Diagnose all six bridges

Start from evidence. A background suggests an order and likely strengths; it does not exempt you from any diagnostic, including quality and resilience. Select demonstrated only after meeting the evidence standard below and recording the basis. Self-assessment is provisional until challenged by a reviewer.

Unknown and practice-required bridges contribute to progress. Demonstrated bridges are waived from the denominator. Preserve the evidence for every waiver.

B1

Code to runtime

First principle: A request crosses browser, HTTP, process, handler and database boundaries. A lost response does not tell you whether the transaction committed.

Guided practice: Start the local lab with python3 learning-paths/software-architect/practice/reservation-lab.py --serve. Open the printed loopback URL. Inspect POST /reserve in the Network panel and match its request key to the terminal trace. Follow do_POST → reserve → transaction → response in the source. Retry the same key, try another key, then stop the server.

Evidence to demonstrate: Annotate one request diagram and match a request to its trace. Explain connection failure, invalid input, stock rejection, database failure and a lost response after commit.

If you need support: If lost, inspect one successful response before reading the entire script. Use the setup and troubleshooting steps in the practice guide.

Full walkthrough and selected reading
B2

APIs, data and distributed failure

First principle: Correctness requires explicit invariants, operation identities and commit boundaries; asynchronous delivery can still duplicate work.

Guided practice: Predict two orders competing for one item, then run the lab without --serve. Follow the conditional update and request/outbox transaction. Inspect interruption before commit and interruption after the consumer effect. Draw synchronous and durable workflow alternatives.

Evidence to demonstrate: Show no oversell, stable retry, rollback and one effect after duplicate delivery. Draw legal states and explain why the receipt simulation does not prove safe external payments.

If you need support: Write down what each participant knows after each commit. Never interpret a timeout as a confirmed decline.

Full walkthrough and selected reading
B3

Quality and resilience

First principle: A quality scenario must be specific enough for another person to construct a falsifying test.

Guided practice: Convert fast, secure, available, easy to change and recoverable into six-field scenarios. Include source, stimulus, environment, artefact, response and response measure. Define the measured population and time window. Execute one failure experiment and revise one scenario after review.

Evidence to demonstrate: Five complete scenarios, one actual failure result and one documented revision. Missing thresholds or an unspecified workload require more practice.

If you need support: Use the complete patient-record scenario in the practice guide as a model, then choose different targets appropriate to your own case.

Full walkthrough and selected reading
B4

Business and domain framing

First principle: A bounded context owns a coherent meaning; an aggregate protects an invariant. Neither automatically requires its own service.

Guided practice: Map promise, reservation, payment and fulfilment. Define what available means in each context. Compare central reservation authority with per-location allocation. Add a two-hour offline store and identify the allocation or accepted oversell rule.

Evidence to demonstrate: A capability map, glossary, context map, invariant ownership and two viable boundary options. Explain every ownership boundary.

If you need support: Begin with one business transaction and ask who may change each fact. Do not derive services by copying table names.

Full walkthrough and selected reading
B5

Cloud, security and operations

First principle: A production design needs identities, failure domains, recoverability, operating ownership and cost assumptions.

Guided practice: Transform the local lab into a hypothetical deployment. Draw user, service and operator identities. Compare failure domains, write SLO/RTO/RPO scenarios, plan a separate-target restore and estimate total operating cost. Identify controls the local lab does not implement.

Evidence to demonstrate: Deployment/trust diagram, access matrix, failure table, cost range and a restore protocol. Only mark a restore verified if it was actually executed and measured.

If you need support: Separate access, availability and recovery questions. Use the worked cost calculation and selected reliability reading.

Full walkthrough and selected reading
B6

Communication and review

First principle: A view answers an audience question; an ADR preserves a decision and the evidence that might reverse it.

Guided practice: Create context/container views and a retry sequence. Write an ADR with credible alternatives and triggers. Explain it in two minutes to a product audience and ten minutes to an engineer, then have the reviewer explain it back.

Evidence to demonstrate: Three understandable views, a complete ADR, feedback and a revision. Any unexplained arrow or untraceable component requires review.

If you need support: Use the worked ADR as a structure, not an answer. Label protocols and distinguish model boundaries from running units.

Unit 1 · Think · complete at your learning pace

Problem framing, stakeholders and quality attributes

An architect does not start with boxes. Start with outcomes, constraints and measurable qualities; only then choose structure.

01
Mentor briefing

Concepts before the exercise

Architecture decision

A decision that shapes important qualities, has a high cost of change, or constrains many later choices.

Quality attribute scenario

A testable statement with source, stimulus, environment, affected artefact, response and measurable response.

Stakeholder concern

What a person or group needs protected: revenue, safety, delivery speed, auditability, usability or operability.

Utility tree

A ranked map connecting business drivers to quality attributes and concrete scenarios.

Visual memory anchor
Scenario brief

National patient access portal

Read all six fields before proposing technology. This is what the candidate is being asked to solve.

Situation

A public healthcare organisation is replacing several regional portals with one service for records, appointments, prescriptions and secure clinician messaging.

Business outcome

Reduce call-centre demand by 30%, give patients consistent digital access and support national policy without disrupting clinical work.

Current state

Seven regional platforms, inconsistent identity checks, nightly data synchronisation and no shared availability target.

Constraints

Sensitive health data; accessibility obligations; 24/7 access; legacy hospital systems; phased rollout; fixed first-year budget.

Decision to make

Which qualities are architecture-driving, how will they be measured, and which stakeholder conflicts require an explicit decision?

Expected output

Stakeholder map, utility tree, six measurable scenarios and three trade-off statements.

Worked example: weak architecture

Looks technical, but is not defensible

“Use microservices on Kubernetes with a NoSQL database because the portal must scale.” Security, availability and accessibility are described only as “high”.

  • Technology chosen before the problem is measured.
  • No evidence that independent deployment is needed.
  • “High security” cannot be tested or used in a review.
  • Clinical, patient, operations and compliance concerns are mixed together.
Worked example: good-enough architecture

Fit for this context, not universally “best”

First rank privacy, availability, accessibility and integration. Define measurable scenarios—for example, emergency record access remains available during one regional integration outage, for authorised reads of previously synchronised records at 200 requests/s, with p95 read latency below 500 ms and at least 99.9% success over a 15-minute test (assumed teaching targets requiring clinical agreement). Use these scenarios to compare a modular platform with separately deployable integration adapters.

  • Business and clinical risks lead the design.
  • Architecture can be evaluated before implementation.
  • The design isolates unstable legacy integrations without forcing every capability into a microservice.
  • Trade-offs are visible to stakeholders.
Explicit trade-off ledger

What is gained, what is sacrificed, how risk is managed

ChoiceWhat it buysWhat it costsArchitect response
Strong identity checksReduces account takeover and privacy riskAdds friction for vulnerable usersUse step-up authentication only for sensitive actions and provide assisted recovery.
Central platformConsistent experience and governanceLarger blast radius and migration riskUse regional isolation cells and progressive rollout.

Unit 2 · Think + Design · complete at your learning pace

Architecture styles and evidence-based trade-offs

Styles are bundles of trade-offs, not maturity levels. The most sophisticated architecture is the simplest one that protects the important qualities.

02
Mentor briefing

Concepts before the exercise

Monolith

One deployment unit. It can still have clean modules and strong internal boundaries.

Modular monolith

One deployment with explicit domain modules, dependency rules and separate ownership inside the codebase.

Service-based

A small number of coarse-grained services, often a pragmatic middle between one deployable and microservices.

Microservices

Independent services and data ownership that buy team and deployment autonomy at the cost of distributed-system complexity.

Visual memory anchor
Scenario brief

Regulated payments startup

Read all six fields before proposing technology. This is what the candidate is being asked to solve.

Situation

A five-person engineering team must launch merchant payments in 16 weeks and expects uncertain growth.

Business outcome

Enter the market quickly, pass a security review and support the first 200 merchants without creating a dead-end platform.

Current state

Greenfield product, limited operations experience and one cloud account.

Constraints

Small team; card data scope must be minimised; strong audit trail; on-call rota of two people; limited budget.

Decision to make

Choose an initial architecture style and define the evidence that would justify later decomposition.

Expected output

Weighted decision matrix, proposed boundaries, two ADRs and three evolution triggers.

Worked example: weak architecture

Looks technical, but is not defensible

Create 18 microservices, separate databases, Kafka, service mesh and Kubernetes from day one because payments require scale.

  • The team cannot own the operational surface.
  • Distributed transactions and debugging delay market entry.
  • Service boundaries are guesses before the domain is learned.
  • Compliance scope may expand rather than shrink.
Worked example: good-enough architecture

Fit for this context, not universally “best”

Use a modular monolith for merchant, payment intent, ledger and reporting, with a managed payment provider to reduce card-data scope. Keep an outbox and explicit module boundaries so high-change or independently scaled capabilities can later be extracted.

  • Fits team size and delivery deadline.
  • Keeps transaction boundaries simple.
  • Preserves an evolution path with measurable triggers.
  • Moves commodity compliance burden to a specialist provider.
Explicit trade-off ledger

What is gained, what is sacrificed, how risk is managed

ChoiceWhat it buysWhat it costsArchitect response
Modular monolithFast delivery, simpler transactions and debuggingSingle deployment and shared scaling unitAccept now; enforce dependency rules and module ownership.
Managed payments providerReduced PCI scope and faster launchVendor fees and dependencyAccept while volumes are low; retain provider abstraction for leverage.
Outbox/event publicationReliable integration and future extractionMore schema and operational workUse only for cross-module and external events, not every state change.

Unit 3 · Design · complete at your learning pace

Scalability, availability and performance

Scale is a workload plus a target, not a diagram full of boxes. Find the hot path, estimate it, remove shared bottlenecks and define degradation.

03
Mentor briefing

Concepts before the exercise

Workload model

Reads, writes, payload size, concurrency, peaks, growth and geographic distribution.

Stateless compute

Requests can run on any healthy instance because durable state lives elsewhere.

Caching

Trading freshness and invalidation complexity for lower latency and reduced origin load.

Resilience

Containing failure through timeout, retry budget, circuit breaker, bulkhead, back-pressure and graceful degradation.

Visual memory anchor
Scenario brief

Global product catalogue and price service

Read all six fields before proposing technology. This is what the candidate is being asked to solve.

Situation

A retailer exposes catalogue, price and availability to web, mobile, stores and partner marketplaces.

Business outcome

Keep customer browsing responsive during campaigns and prevent incorrect checkout prices.

Current state

One relational database serves catalogue editing, search, pricing and checkout reads. Campaigns cause lock contention and timeouts.

Constraints

150k read requests/sec peak; 2k updates/sec; prices must be correct at checkout; search can be seconds stale; 99.99% browse availability.

Decision to make

Separate read paths, define consistency per use case and design overload behaviour.

Expected output

Capacity estimate, request-path diagram, cache policy, failure-mode table and SLOs.

Worked example: weak architecture

Looks technical, but is not defensible

Add a larger database and cache every endpoint for one hour. Retry failed requests three times everywhere.

  • Vertical scaling leaves one failure domain.
  • Uniform cache policy ignores price correctness.
  • Retries at multiple layers multiply load even when each caller has a bounded attempt count; share a total deadline and retry budget.
  • No plan for degraded search, stale inventory or cache stampede.
Worked example: good-enough architecture

Fit for this context, not universally “best”

Separate authoring from read-optimised projections. Put catalogue/search behind CDN and distributed cache; validate price and reservable inventory on the checkout path. Use event-driven projection updates, request coalescing, timeout budgets and controlled degradation.

  • Different consistency needs receive different designs.
  • Hot reads are removed from the transactional store.
  • Checkout correctness is protected.
  • Failure behaviour is designed rather than accidental.
Explicit trade-off ledger

What is gained, what is sacrificed, how risk is managed

ChoiceWhat it buysWhat it costsArchitect response
Aggressive cachingVery low browse latencyStale product and inventory dataCache static catalogue longer; use short TTL/event invalidation for availability.
Read projectionsIndependent scaling and query optimisationEventual consistency and reconciliationDisplay freshness timestamp and reconcile failed events.
Graceful degradationKeeps core shopping journey availableSome features are temporarily reducedDisable recommendations before catalogue or checkout.

Unit 4 · Design · complete at your learning pace

APIs, integration and distributed workflows

A distributed workflow is a failure protocol. Draw the unhappy path before selecting choreography, orchestration or synchronous calls.

04
Mentor briefing

Concepts before the exercise

Synchronous API

Caller waits for a response; simple to understand but couples latency and availability.

Event

A durable fact that has happened; consumers may react independently.

Saga

A sequence of local transactions with explicit compensation or recovery when later steps fail.

Idempotency

Repeating the same operation produces no unintended additional effect—essential for retries and duplicate messages.

Visual memory anchor
Scenario brief

Omnichannel order placement

Read all six fields before proposing technology. This is what the candidate is being asked to solve.

Situation

Customers can buy online for home delivery, click-and-collect or ship-from-store. Payment, inventory, fraud and fulfilment are separate systems.

Business outcome

Accept valid orders quickly without overselling or charging customers for orders that cannot be fulfilled.

Current state

A synchronous chain calls seven systems; one slow warehouse API causes checkout failure. Operations manually repair partial orders.

Constraints

External payment provider; duplicate messages possible; stores can be offline; partial fulfilment supported; complete audit trail required.

Decision to make

Choose the workflow coordination model and define state, idempotency, compensation and operator recovery.

Expected output

State machine, sequence diagrams for three paths, event/API contracts, one ADR and recovery runbook.

Worked example: weak architecture

Looks technical, but is not defensible

Checkout calls fraud, payment, inventory, warehouse, loyalty, email and analytics in sequence inside one request. Roll back everything on any error.

  • A required synchronous chain couples availability to its dependencies. A product calculation is only a simplified model under suitable independence assumptions; correlated failures and degraded modes change it.
  • These independent providers do not share one ACID transaction; design for partial completion rather than assuming distributed rollback.
  • Timeouts leave ambiguous state.
  • Analytics and notification unnecessarily block revenue.
Worked example: good-enough architecture

Fit for this context, not universally “best”

Create an order with a durable state machine. Orchestrate payment authorisation and inventory reservation with idempotency keys and compensations. Publish order events through a transactional outbox; notifications and analytics consume asynchronously. Provide an exception queue for human recovery.

  • The critical business process has one visible owner.
  • Ambiguous and partial states are represented explicitly.
  • Non-critical work no longer blocks checkout.
  • Operators have evidence and repair controls.

Unit 5 · Design · complete at your learning pace

Data architecture and information ownership

Choose data stores from access patterns, consistency and ownership. “One source of truth” should mean authority, not one database for every workload.

05
Mentor briefing

Concepts before the exercise

Operational data

Supports current business transactions and needs predictable correctness and latency.

Analytical data

Optimised for history, aggregation, exploration and model training.

Data product

A domain-owned, discoverable dataset or stream with quality, schema, access and service expectations.

Change data capture

Publishes database changes so downstream systems can update without querying the source repeatedly.

Visual memory anchor
Scenario brief

Retail inventory intelligence

Read all six fields before proposing technology. This is what the candidate is being asked to solve.

Situation

A chain operates hundreds of stores, distribution centres and an online channel. It needs reliable available-to-promise inventory and demand forecasting.

Business outcome

Reduce overselling, improve fulfilment choice and lower excess stock.

Current state

ERP inventory is exported nightly; stores send files; analysts query replicas of the operational database.

Constraints

Store connectivity is intermittent; inventory events can arrive late or duplicate; finance needs reconciled daily figures; customer channels need near-real-time availability.

Decision to make

Define system-of-record ownership, event ingestion, operational projections, analytical platform and reconciliation.

Expected output

Data-domain map, source-to-serving flow, schema rules, freshness SLOs and data-quality controls.

Worked example: weak architecture

Looks technical, but is not defensible

Put ERP, web events, sensor data, reports and ML features into one enterprise data lake and call it the single source of truth.

  • A lake does not own business transactions.
  • Fresh operational decisions become dependent on batch ingestion.
  • Unclear ownership produces untrusted data.
  • Security and retention needs are mixed.
Worked example: good-enough architecture

Fit for this context, not universally “best”

Keep authoritative stock movements in operational domains. Publish versioned inventory events into a streaming platform, build an available-to-promise projection for channels, and land immutable events in a lakehouse/warehouse for forecasting and finance reconciliation. Assign owners and data contracts.

  • Operational and analytical needs are separated.
  • Authority is explicit.
  • Late and duplicate data can be reconciled.
  • Consumers receive defined freshness and quality guarantees.

Unit 6 · Design + Think · complete at your learning pace

Security architecture and resilience engineering

Security is a set of trust decisions. Resilience is a set of failure decisions. Both must be drawn through data flows and tested as scenarios.

06
Mentor briefing

Concepts before the exercise

Trust boundary

A point where identity, privilege, ownership or control changes and must be verified.

Threat modelling

Systematically ask what can go wrong, what to do, whether it is enough and what evidence exists.

Least privilege

Grant only the permissions required, for the shortest useful time and smallest scope.

RTO / RPO

Maximum acceptable recovery time and maximum acceptable data loss measured in time.

Visual memory anchor
Scenario brief

Digital wallet and refunds platform

Read all six fields before proposing technology. This is what the candidate is being asked to solve.

Situation

A retailer is introducing a stored-value wallet, instant refunds and partner gift cards across web, mobile and stores.

Business outcome

Improve retention and refund speed while preventing fraud and protecting regulated payment and personal data.

Current state

Shared service credentials, broad database access and perimeter-only security. Incident evidence is incomplete.

Constraints

PCI-related controls; GDPR; third-party gift-card providers; store devices; fraud decisions under 300 ms; audit retention.

Decision to make

Define trust boundaries, identity flows, data protection, abuse controls and recovery objectives.

Expected output

Data-flow diagram, STRIDE table, top-risk treatment, authentication/authorisation flow and resilience plan.

Worked example: weak architecture

Looks technical, but is not defensible

All internal services are trusted after entering the VPC. A shared admin JWT is stored in configuration. Encryption terminates at the gateway.

  • Network location is treated as identity.
  • One credential creates broad blast radius.
  • East-west traffic and data stores remain exposed.
  • No separation between user, service and operator privileges.
Worked example: good-enough architecture

Fit for this context, not universally “best”

Authenticate users with OIDC and step-up for sensitive actions; give services workload identities and scoped authorisation; encrypt in transit and at rest; tokenise payment identifiers; isolate wallet ledger; add fraud rate limits, immutable audit events and tested recovery objectives.

  • Trust is explicit at every boundary.
  • Privileges and blast radius are reduced.
  • Sensitive data scope is constrained.
  • Detection and recovery are part of the design.

Architecture governance, influence and evolutionary fitness

Mentor briefing

Concepts before the exercise

Architecture review

A structured conversation that tests drivers, options, risks and evidence—not a design approval theatre.

Fitness function

An automated or repeatable measure that checks whether an architecture characteristic remains within an acceptable range.

Tech radar

A transparent way to communicate technology adoption positions and evidence.

Conway’s Law

System structure tends to mirror communication structure; team design and architecture therefore interact.

Visual memory anchor
Scenario brief

Multi-team commerce platform governance

Read all six fields before proposing technology. This is what the candidate is being asked to solve.

Situation

Twelve product teams independently choose frameworks, event formats, observability tools and cloud patterns. Delivery is fast but incidents and integration costs are rising.

Business outcome

Preserve team autonomy while reducing duplicated platforms, security drift and cross-team failure.

Current state

Central architecture board meets monthly and rejects designs late. Teams bypass it to meet deadlines.

Constraints

Different domains and workload needs; limited central team; high change rate; acquisitions introduce more technology.

Design lightweight governance, golden paths, exception handling and measurable architecture fitness.

Worked example: weak architecture

Looks technical, but is not defensible

  • Central team becomes a bottleneck.

  • Teams retain choice inside explicit boundaries.
  • Risk is measured continuously.
  • Exceptions become managed decisions rather than hidden divergence.

Capstone mobilisation workshop

You have received an ambiguous industry brief and must turn it into a reviewable architecture proposal within an agreed effort budget with explicit review checkpoints.

Industry capstone · evidence-led architecture engagement

NorthStar Retail: Omnichannel Order & Inventory Modernisation

Fictional but industry-derived composite scenario. The business problem is synthesised from current official reference architectures for distributed order management, web commerce, real-time supply chain and demand planning. The company and numerical targets below are pedagogical assumptions, not claims about a real retailer.

Company and problem

Business context

NorthStar Retail operates 420 stores, 8 distribution centres, an ecommerce site, a mobile app and a growing third-party marketplace across the UK and EU.

Inventory is synchronised in batches every 15 minutes. Customers see unavailable stock, stores reject click-and-collect orders, and operations manually reconcile payments, reservations and fulfilment. The 17-year-old order platform has a shared database and eight-week release cycle.

Stakeholders

CustomersStore colleaguesEcommerce productOrder operationsWarehouse operationsCustomer serviceFinanceSecurity and privacyPlatform/SREData and forecastingMarketplace sellersExecutive sponsor
Success and constraints

What the architecture must achieve

  • Reduce cancelled orders caused by inaccurate availability by 60%.
  • Support click-and-collect, ship-from-store and marketplace fulfilment.
  • Provide order promise responses below 300 ms p95 during normal operation.
  • Keep stores able to trade for up to two hours during network loss.
  • Deliver the first production capability in six months and complete modernisation in 18 months.

Hard constraints

  • Black Friday traffic may reach 12× a normal peak.
  • No big-bang replacement; old and new platforms must coexist.
  • Payment data scope must remain controlled; GDPR applies to customer and behavioural data.
  • Legacy ERP, warehouse and store systems cannot all be changed in the first year.
  • Inventory events may be late, duplicated or arrive out of order.
  • Finance needs reconciled daily figures; customer channels need near-real-time availability.
  • Target RTO is 30 minutes for customer ordering and RPO is 5 minutes for order state.
  • Cloud spending must be allocated by business capability and reviewed monthly.
Candidate brief

Your role

You are the lead solution architect for the first modernisation increment. You must produce a defensible target and migration architecture without pretending every unknown is resolved. Present two viable options, select one for the first 18 months, expose risks and identify evidence that would change your decision.

Not expected: a vendor-service catalogue, a perfect final-state design or code for every component. Expected: traceable judgement, credible failure behaviour, measurable qualities, evolution and communication.

Capstone assessment

Score inspected evidence; enforce every gate

Apply this rubric to the complete capstone. Daily units use their own acceptance checklists and review scope. A score is a judgement of submitted learning evidence, not a professional certification or a mapping to a SFIA responsibility level.

Scoring anchors: use 0 for absent or invalid evidence; 1–39% for fragmented evidence; 40–59% for developing evidence; 60–79% for acceptable evidence; 80–100% for strong evidence. Use whole points and record the reason. Each dimension needs at least 60% (rounded up), with stricter floors of 10/15 each for technical coherence and security/operability. A high total cannot override a critical failure

Any unresolved material invariant violation (for example overselling beyond an agreed allocation rule or duplicate charging), unauthorised sensitive action, unrecoverable migration, invented result or concealed uncertainty blocks a pass.
