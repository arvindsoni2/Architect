Prepare in three passes

  1. Match: identify the five strongest requirements in the advert; match each to a story and a decision you actually made.
  2. Explain: rehearse a 90-second answer; prepare the artefact, measurement and alternative for follow-up questions.
  3. Challenge: practise a recovery or prioritisation scenario, a setback and a credibility probe. Ask what the panel could challenge in your wording.

If only two hours remain, spend 20 minutes decoding the advert, 50 minutes on three stories, 25 minutes on a scenario, 15 minutes on motivation and questions, and 10 minutes checking logistics.

Use this handbook to explain your judgement. Choose relevant methods and artefacts, connect them to what you did, and show how the outcome changed. Definitions live here; role guides apply them to different responsibilities. Historical facts live in the evidence bank.

Decode the advert before rehearsing

For each important requirement, write: intended outcome; expected decision authority; user or stakeholder; matching example; gap or adjacent experience; likely follow-up. Identify whether the employer needs a product decision-maker, a delivery leader or a project controller. Titles vary, so test the remit with the recruiter.

Distinguish three levels honestly: direct experience, adjacent experience, and knowledge you would apply with support. Certifications demonstrate learning; formal role accountabilities still need examples.

Opening and motivation answers

An opening or motivation answer needs a different structure from a past-experience story. The following are coaching devices, not Accenture-mandated methods or claims of superior hiring outcomes. Use the panel’s requested format where one is supplied.

Present → Past → Future

Also called Now → Then → Next. Aim for roughly 60–90 seconds: professional identity and relevant strengths now, one or two selected proofs, then the next step and why this role fits. Present describes your current professional position; it does not require claiming current employment.

ElementAnswer promptAvoid
PresentWho are you professionally, and which strength matters here?Claiming a former employer is current
PastWhich one or two experiences demonstrate that strength?Listing every employer, date and technology
FutureWhat do you want next, why this remit, and what will you contribute?A generic wish for career growth

Template: “I’m a [professional identity] with experience in [relevant work]. My strength is [role-relevant contribution]. At [example], I [actual action or responsibility], resulting in [defensible outcome]. More recently, [second relevant proof]. I’m now looking to [next step], and this role fits because [specific remit and contribution].”

Use the Accenture opening for a factual adaptation. Keep your employment timeline available for follow-up without making it the whole introduction.

Company → Role → Fit

CRF is a convenient label for this motivation structure. Give one or two specific company reasons, explain the work that attracts you, then connect your evidence and contribution. When asked “why now?”, make the intended next step explicit; a proof point alone does not answer timing.

ElementAnswer promptUseful check
CompanyWhy this organisation or client environment?Is the reason specific and supported by the advert or a current source?
RoleWhich responsibilities or problems attract you?Have you explained the remit rather than repeated the title?
Fit and why nowWhat proves you can contribute, and why is this the next step?Have you balanced what you offer with what you want to learn?

Template: “I’m interested in [company] because [specific reason]. This role stands out because [responsibilities or challenge]. It fits my experience in [evidence], where [actual contribution and result]. I now want to [next step], and would bring [contribution] to [relevant client or product outcome].”

If the questions are separate, focus each answer: company motivation can use mission, market and credible current momentum; role motivation can use responsibilities, impact and development. These are optional prompts, not facts to manufacture. Do not claim a particular client assignment or repeat unsupported company news.

Point → Evidence → Explanation → Link

PEEL is useful for a values or professional-opinion question. State your point, give a short real example, explain why it supports your view, then link it to the vacancy. It is not a replacement for a full behavioural story when the panel asks for one. For a knowledge question, a simpler Point → Example → Implication answer is usually enough.

Template: “[Value or principle] matters to me. In [real example], [actual contribution or outcome]. That taught me [relevant judgement]. In this role I would apply it by [specific contribution].”

Teaching cases and personal answers

Fictional worked answers can demonstrate an entire response, including plausible numbers. Label the case at its start, explain the metric and then replace the details with actual evidence for personal rehearsal. A made-up percentage teaches arithmetic, not a result you achieved. If personal actions are missing, keep a complete fictional teaching answer beside the factual adaptation prompts; do not fill your historical record with the invented actions.

The Accenture PO playbook uses this approach and names the structure for each of its 15 core questions. In a hypothetical, use future language and proposed measures. In a behavioural answer, establish personal actions and observed results. A good answer need not end with a dramatic success; an honest stop decision or useful correction can show judgement.

Behavioural answers

For a question about something you have done, choose STAR or PEARL, then make your responsibility, decision and evidence explicit. These are ways to organise an answer, not substitutes for relevant experience. Accenture recommends STAR; Jackie Bavaro describes PEARL for product interviews. Use the employer's requested format when one is specified.

  1. Give enough context to explain the difficulty.
  2. State your remit and authority.
  3. Describe what you personally decided, the alternative, the trade-off and the action.
  4. Explain the result, its measurement and any limit to attribution.
  5. State what you changed afterwards or would do differently.

Prepare a 90-second version and a deeper follow-up. An artefact matters because it enabled a decision: a RACI clarified approvals, a RAID log surfaced a blocking dependency, or a prototype overturned an assumption. Naming a method alone does not establish competence.

Use several stories across an interview. Recent Natoora examples show current product and delivery capability; Northern Powergrid adds field adoption and infrastructure complexity; banking examples add regulated and supplier experience. Choose relevance over recency when a question demands a specific kind of evidence.

Add a short reflection when the question concerns failure, feedback or growth. The four letters do not require omitting learning. Spend enough time on action to establish ownership; do not let background consume the answer. A result may be a justified stop decision, a contained failure or an improved process, as well as a successful launch.

PEARL makes insight visible in product, strategy and improvement stories. An epiphany can be a carefully developed observation; do not invent a dramatic breakthrough. Bavaro's 2025 explanation particularly uses learning for growth stories. Keep reflection relevant rather than attaching a generic lesson to every answer.

Choice: STAR suits a clear responsibility/action/outcome account; PEARL is useful when the important contribution was reframing the problem. Both can show senior judgement. The sources reviewed provide guidance, not comparative evidence that one acronym produces better hiring outcomes.

Same teaching case, two structures

Fictional example, not Arvind's employment history: an onboarding team was asked to build a tutorial. Research and funnel data instead showed confusion about optional form fields. A bounded change improved pilot completion while support demand remained stable. All actions and outcomes in the next two paragraphs belong to this invented example.

STAR: “Our onboarding journey lost users before submission. As PO, I was responsible for improving completion while preserving required data collection. I examined the drop-off with the analyst and observed users with the researcher. We found that optional fields appeared compulsory. I compared a tutorial with a clearer, shorter form, recommended the form change and agreed a limited pilot with engineering and the accountable sponsor. We checked completion and support demand for the pilot cohort. Completion improved without worsening support demand. I learned to investigate the journey before treating a requested feature as the solution.”

PEARL: “Users were abandoning onboarding, and the proposed solution was a tutorial. The insight from observation and funnel data was that they misunderstood optional fields; instructions would leave that friction in place. I recommended simplifying the form, explained the alternative to the sponsor and worked with design and engineering on a bounded pilot that preserved mandatory data collection. Pilot completion improved while support demand remained stable. I took forward a habit of testing the problem behind a feature request before committing roadmap capacity.”

For your own version, use the evidence bank and supply only your actual insight, action and result. The fictional example is a structure demonstration, not a script to adopt as experience.

Scenario answers

For a hypothetical, explain assumptions → immediate protection → diagnosis → options → decision → sequence → measures. This is the handbook's practice sequence, not an employer-mandated acronym. Ask two or three material questions about users, impact, constraints and authority, then proceed with stated assumptions if further information is unavailable.

  1. Clarify the decision: establish the desired outcome, deadline, affected users, missing information and your authority. Distinguish a mandatory obligation from a stakeholder preference.
  2. Protect where needed: contain immediate harm to users, production, security or people while investigating. A low-adoption case may need research; an active incident needs action now.
  3. Diagnose with evidence: explain the data, user research and specialist input you would seek. Separate facts from hypotheses; do not assume the first explanation is the cause.
  4. Compare realistic options: include a smaller scope, changed sequence, bounded pilot, pause or defer where relevant. Compare value, feasibility, time, cost, dependencies and risk.
  5. Recommend and secure the decision: choose a provisional course, explain the trade-off and identify any approval beyond your remit. Say what evidence would change your view.
  6. Sequence execution: name the immediate step, owners, dependency or escalation and review point. Separate today's containment from the subsequent improvement.
  7. Measure and adapt: define the expected outcome, baseline needed and guardrails. These are proposed success criteria; do not report them as achieved results.

STAR and PEARL describe a completed experience most naturally. In a hypothetical you can use their logic, but a predicted result remains a target or test. Saying “this would increase adoption by 30%” without evidence weakens the answer.

Worked release scenario — illustrative, not historical

Prompt: the client wants a reporting dashboard in two weeks. Research shows users cannot complete the core workflow, and security has identified a release blocker.

A concise response: “I would clarify the launch commitment, the users affected and the security finding with the relevant specialist. I would not recommend releasing the affected functionality until the blocker was resolved, or a verified workaround satisfied the required control and received the proper approval. I would compare fixing the core workflow and deferring the dashboard, a smaller dashboard alongside those fixes, and changing the launch date. Assuming the dashboard is discretionary and capacity is constrained, I would recommend prioritising the usable, secure core journey. I would bring the value and scope recommendation to the client PO or sponsor, with engineering owning the technical plan. We would make backlog changes, acceptance and operational readiness visible. Before expansion, I would check workflow completion, support demand and control verification. A contractual reporting obligation or new evidence about impact could change the scope decision.”

Follow-up probes: What if the client refuses? Who may accept residual risk? Which work can continue in parallel? What if research is incomplete? A strong follow-up distinguishes legitimate risk acceptance from permission to breach a mandatory control, and continues useful work while a decision is escalated.

If a team is six weeks late after losing its lead, address immediate production, contractual or people risks straight away. Diagnose in parallel through team and stakeholder conversations, current commitments, delivery evidence and dependencies. Establish a small stabilisation plan with owners and dates; then agree a realistic forecast and improvement experiments. A first week of listening must not delay urgent protection.

Define progress in outcomes: reduced blocked time, clearer decisions, a reliable forecast, lower incident exposure or restored client confidence. Avoid promising that a particular ceremony, framework or headcount increase will repair every situation.

Applying the toolkit

Decision rights and stakeholders. RACI means Responsible, Accountable, Consulted and Informed. Map a small set of consequential decisions, not every activity. Confirm one accountable decision owner and the route when a decision exceeds their authority. A power/interest map can guide communication, but include affected users with little organisational power. In an answer, explain which delayed or duplicated decision the artefact resolved.

Prioritisation. MoSCoW distinguishes Must, Should, Could and Won't for the agreed time horizon. A Must requires a clear consequence if omitted; “the stakeholder wants it” is insufficient. WSJF compares cost of delay with job duration or a size proxy. In the SAFe formulation, cost of delay combines relative user/business value, time criticality and risk reduction/opportunity enablement. Dependencies and mandatory controls still constrain the order. Kano can help discuss basic needs, performance expectations and possible delights; validate those categories with users.

Illustrative WSJF exercise: option A has relative cost of delay 12 and size 3, giving 4; option B has cost of delay 15 and size 5, giving 3. On that limited evidence A comes first. Explain the scoring uncertainty, whether B enables A and whether either is mandatory. This is a reasoning exercise, not a historical prioritisation decision.

Risk and forecasting. RAID records Risks, Assumptions, Issues and Dependencies. A risk needs a response, owner and trigger; an issue needs action now. A pre-mortem asks why a future failure might happen and converts plausible causes into controls or experiments. Critical-path analysis identifies the dependency chain controlling completion under the current plan. Resource constraints or uncertainty can change it. A burn-up separates completed work from total scope; widening scope can explain why a stable completion rate still misses the date.

Change and coaching. ADKAR considers Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability and Reinforcement. Diagnose the actual barrier: another training session does not fix a lack of time or tools to use the skill. For organisation-wide change, a coalition, clear purpose, removal of practical barriers, visible wins and reinforcement can help; do not present change as a rigid sequence that everyone experiences identically. Adapt coaching to the task and person's support needs, with feedback and a review point.

Facilitation. In 1-2-4-All, start with individual reflection, then pairs, fours and whole-group sharing to widen participation. “What, so what, now what” separates observation, interpretation and next action. Five Whys can produce a causal hypothesis, but verify it against evidence and avoid forcing every incident into one cause. Close with an owner, action and review date. The interview value is the better decision or changed behaviour, not the workshop name.

Commercial contracts. Time and materials, fixed-price and managed-service arrangements create different incentives and obligations. Establish the actual contract before discussing scope, capacity or billing. Prepare one example where a change process, milestone acceptance or forecast enabled a fair decision. Claim margin or utilisation ownership only if it was genuinely part of your remit.

Scrum, Kanban and scaled delivery

In Scrum, the Product Owner is accountable for maximising value and effective Product Backlog management. Delegation does not transfer that accountability. The team collaborates on the Sprint Goal; Developers select and plan the work they can complete. The Scrum Master supports effectiveness and self-management. A review inspects outcomes and adapts direction; it is not merely a status demonstration.

Use Kanban thinking to understand work in progress, throughput, cycle time, ageing work and blocked time. Make workflow policies explicit, then test a change against the actual bottleneck. Local productivity gains can leave end-to-end flow unchanged.

For scaled delivery, explain the underlying problem: objectives, dependencies, capacity, integration, decision rights and shared outcomes. In a SAFe context, learn the employer's actual ART, PI planning and backlog arrangements. Product Management leads strategy and roadmapping across the ART; team-level ownership has a different remit. Do not retrospectively label multi-team work as a formal SAFe implementation.

Public sector and regulated work

Know the distinction between the GDS Service Standard, Technology Code of Practice, user-centred design practice and formal assessment experience. Discovery investigates the problem and whether to proceed; alpha tests risky assumptions through prototypes; beta builds and tests the service; live includes continuing improvement and operations. Retirement is also part of lifecycle ownership.

Current government Service Manual guidance specifies WCAG 2.2 AA; check the assignment’s applicable requirements and test with representative users as well as automated tools. For a government service, consider whole journeys across channels, accessibility and assisted digital support, privacy, security, performance and operational reliability. The Service Manual identifies cost per transaction, user satisfaction, completion and digital take-up as service KPIs; add measures for the specific service. Confirm which standards and publication requirements apply to the assignment.

In banking or utilities, use a real regulatory or operational constraint rather than claiming government delivery by association. In an AI service, consider data suitability, privacy, evaluation, user recourse, exception handling and monitoring. Define release thresholds with the relevant specialists and accountable owner.

Rehearsal and self-review

Score each answer from 0–2 for relevance, personal ownership, judgement, evidence, calibration and clarity. A zero identifies a concrete rehearsal gap. Ask a partner to probe your metric, the rejected alternative and who held authority. This is a practice rubric, not an Accenture or Civil Service scoring scale.

For a scenario, score clarity of assumptions, evidence sought, options compared, decision/authority, execution sequence and measures/guardrails. Use the same 0–2 scale: absent, mentioned, or explained with a consequence. This is a self-review aid; it does not predict a hiring score.

Practice loop: take one role-specific drill, use a minute to outline the decision, then give a two-minute response. Have a partner change one constraint: half the capacity, an unavailable approver, unreliable data or an inflexible deadline. Revise the recommendation aloud. Finish by answering one past-experience question using STAR or PEARL. The timings are rehearsal choices; use the actual interview's allowance.

Common mistakes: asking endless questions without a recommendation; reciting ceremonies; promising an unsupported improvement; spending money or accepting risk without authority; overlooking users and operations; treating every problem as an emergency; and inventing a result for a hypothetical. Take notes as prompts rather than memorising a polished script.

Before an interview, confirm format, panel, case or presentation, duration and logistics. Prepare a specific motivation, three questions, a setback and a truthful current-status answer. Future interview formats are not established by the stages used in an earlier application.

Model answer: "I agree the outcome and its measure before we build, then check it after release. On the GenAI order automation at Natoora the outcome was the time customer support spent keying orders from emails — two to three minutes each. We measured manual effort before and after the London and Paris rollout and saw about a 90% reduction, with exceptions still handled by people. Shipping the feature was the output; the released time was the value."

Q2. Have you used AI to support backlog refinement or product decisions?

Model answer: "Yes, as an assistant, not a decision-maker. I use LLMs to draft story splits and acceptance examples from a feature description, to summarise feedback themes, and to challenge my requirements before refinement — then the team and I check them, because a model will happily write plausible acceptance criteria that miss a real constraint. I don't paste confidential client data into public tools." [1]

Q3. How would you use AI in product discovery?

Model answer: "To move faster between idea and evidence: quick clickable prototypes to test with users, synthesis of interview notes into themes I then verify against the raw notes, and comparing several solution options early. The rule is that AI speeds up learning from users; it doesn't replace talking to them. And I'd always ask whether the problem needs AI at all — rules or an ordinary form often solve it more cheaply." [1]

Q4. Tell me about a time you tested an idea and it didn't work.

Model answer: "In July I tested whether newer local models should replace the writing model in Hatch, my open-source job-search tool. I set hard quality gates first. All 27 runs produced valid output, but no challenger passed the gates, so I kept the existing model. It felt like a non-result, but it saved me shipping a change that looked newer and was not better — and it confirmed the gates were doing their job." For a work example, use the unused-feature setback in B2. [1]

Q5. How do you align stakeholders with competing priorities?

Model answer: Use B1: two launches needed the same senior engineer. "I met each side with options, then ran a joint session where everyone saw the dependency and the options beforehand, tied to company goals, with the CTO available to decide if we couldn't. We agreed to finish GenAI first and delay multiple basket by two to three weeks, because its go-to-market wasn't final. Nobody left surprised." [1]

Q6. How do you help a team deliver value end to end?

Model answer: "Fewer hand-offs and faster feedback. When I joined Natoora, work lived in Trello with no baseline and unpredictable releases. I led the move to Jira and an Agile operating model across engineering, product and design, with CI/CD, feature flags and automated deployments. Time from starting work to release fell from more than five weeks to under three. The tool mattered less than one shared backlog and the release automation." [1]

Q7. What makes a sprint successful?

Model answer: "The Sprint Goal is met with a usable, Done increment, and we learned something that changes the next decision — from users, data or the work itself. Velocity going up is not success; a goal met with an increment nobody uses isn't either." [2]

Q8. When is AI the wrong answer for a product problem?

Model answer: "When the rules are known, the result must be exact or the volume is low. A structured prediction, such as which customers are likely to churn, is usually better served by a conventional model that is cheaper and easier to explain. In my IncidentPilot project the deterministic rules make the diagnosis and the model only ranks hypotheses. I'd start with the simplest thing that meets the outcome and add AI where it measurably helps."

Q9. What do you know about our product and market?

Model answer: Prepare three specifics: who the product serves, one recent change you noticed, and one question you would want to test in your first month. Tie one of them to your evidence. [2]

First 90 days and panel questions

In days 1–30, learn users, strategy, decision rights, team constraints and baseline measures. In days 31–60, improve backlog clarity and test a high-value assumption with a small release. In days 61–90, assess outcome evidence, strengthen rollout/support ownership and adapt the roadmap. Sequence this around the client's existing phase and commitments.

Ask: Who owns product strategy and final priority decisions? How much access does the PO have to users? What has made benefits hard to realise? How are security, UCD and operations involved? What would good performance look like in six months?

Rehearsal: one product story, one rejected priority, one user insight, one metric defence, one coaching example and the scenario above. Use Cracking the PM Career as supplementary preparation; translate its product-career material to this role's backlog, client and delivery context.

Product Manager interview guide

Status: Draft for owner review · Last reviewed: 1 October 2026

Prepare to defend which problems deserve investment, for which users, and how the product can produce sustainable outcomes. The emphasis is broader discovery, strategy and economics. Actual decision rights vary by organisation; establish them before assuming the PM controls funding or the PO only writes stories.

Positioning and evidence

A useful opening: “My product and delivery background has involved improving operational services, adapting an internal platform for external customers and rolling out AI-enabled order processing. I connect user problems with delivery feasibility and commercial consequences. I would bring that experience to product discovery, prioritisation and learning after launch.” State your actual historical titles and the product decisions you personally owned.

Questions to rehearse

  1. How would you build a product strategy? Identify the segment, important unmet need, competing alternatives, organisation's strengths and constraints. State a few strategic choices, an outcome and the assumptions that could invalidate them. Explain what you will stop doing.
  2. How do you discover a valuable problem? Combine user interviews and observation with service data and commercial evidence. Avoid equating a stakeholder feature request with validated demand. Separate desirability, feasibility, viability and operational constraints.
  3. How do you construct a roadmap? Organise around outcomes and problems, make dependencies visible and distinguish near-term commitments from uncertain future options. Set evidence-based review points. A roadmap is not a promise that every item will ship on a fixed date.
  4. How would you measure product-market fit? Specify the market and business model. Look for repeated use, retention, willingness to pay and customer outcomes. Enterprise sales cycles and implementation services change the evidence you can obtain. One successful client is useful learning, not universal proof.
  5. When would you stop a product or feature? Define a hypothesis and threshold before investment. Include strategic fit, opportunity cost, customer commitments, migration and support obligations. Describe a real stop/defer decision if available; otherwise label the example hypothetical.
  6. How do you work with engineering and design? Co-discover the problem and risks, involve engineers early, test alternatives cheaply, and preserve their responsibility for technical design. Explain how learning affects the scope rather than handing over a fixed solution.
  7. How do you make an AI product decision? Compare rules, conventional software and model-based approaches against representative user tasks. Consider error consequences, validation, exception handling, data rights, latency, cost and operational ownership. Use outcome measures alongside model evaluation.
  8. How do you balance growth and reliability? Show the cost of failure, support load, retained customers and the marginal value of another feature. Agree guardrails and choose a sustainable investment mix.

Commercial worked example

For SaaS, explain the opportunity to adapt a proven internal warehouse platform for external customers. The owner-confirmed figures are £28k setup, £1.6k recurring monthly revenue and £29k reported profit in the first five months after rollout. The recurring revenue supported cloud operations, developer support and other expenses. Your responsibility ended after that period; do not extrapolate later profitability.

Discuss one choice about target customer, onboarding scope or reusable capability. State who approved pricing and investment. Prepare the cost basis and customer coverage for a financial follow-up; the guide does not reconstruct missing accounting records. Compare £9k and £7k implementation cost separately from four and two months elapsed delivery time. Scope differences matter to both comparisons.

Product case — illustrative, not historical

“Should the warehouse platform expand to another customer segment?” Start with the decision and ask about segment size, current alternatives, common workflows, buying process and constraints. Interview a small representative group; analyse recurring needs and customisation burden. Test a proposition through demos or a bounded pilot before building a broad feature set. Assess recurring revenue, cloud/support costs, onboarding investment and risk to the core business. Choose proceed, narrow, defer or stop with a clear evidence threshold and accountable sponsor.

If asked for a metric tree, connect the outcome to drivers: successful customer operations depend on activated customers, reliable daily use and task completion. Revenue depends on pricing and retained paying customers; contribution depends on revenue less the relevant costs. Agree definitions before adding targets. No arbitrary percentage target is evidence of strategy.

Credibility probes

What was your hypothesis before delivery? Which users did you speak to? How did an insight change the roadmap? What alternative would have solved the problem? What did you decide personally? Were financial results realised or forecast? Which failure or metric would change your recommendation?

For AI, remember that the confirmed result is a reduction in manual effort during London/Paris rollout. It is not an accuracy measure or proof that a fixed share of orders needed no human work.

Additional scenario drills

These are fictional practice cases. Use the shared scenario method, concentrating on the investment decision and the evidence that could reverse it.

PM1 — acquisition rises but retention falls. A campaign brings more sign-ups, while retained use and support economics deteriorate. Clarify segments, cohort behaviour, activation, pricing and acquisition cost. Investigate whether the campaign attracted unsuitable customers or the journey fails to deliver value. Compare narrowing acquisition, improving onboarding and changing the proposition. Recommend a bounded test with retention, customer outcomes and cost guardrails. Probe: what if a quarter-end revenue target rewards continued acquisition? Weak response: celebrating sign-ups or proposing another feature without identifying the cause.

PM2 — one large customer requests a custom product. A prospect offers attractive revenue if the team builds specialised features. Check strategic fit, reusable demand, delivery/support cost, contractual commitments and opportunity cost. Compare a configurable capability, a priced bounded engagement and declining the request. Recommend an option with the investment/commercial owner and evidence from other customers. Probe: what if sales already promised the date? Weak response: accepting on headline revenue alone or assuming all customisation is undesirable.

First 90 days and panel questions

First understand the market, users, strategy, economics and decision rights. Next validate the riskiest assumption and align a small set of outcome measures. Then recommend roadmap changes using evidence from customers and operations, with engineering/design feasibility and stakeholder agreement.

Ask: Which product choices can this role make? How are discovery and delivery funded? What evidence informs investment reviews? How is support cost represented in product decisions? Which assumption in the current strategy worries the team most?

Questions to rehearse

  1. How do you initiate a project? Confirm the problem, sponsor, business case, measurable benefits, scope boundaries, assumptions, dependencies and governance. Establish a realistic delivery approach and who can accept residual risk or changes.
  2. How do you build a credible plan? Work with delivery experts on work packages, estimates, dependencies, resource constraints and acceptance. Identify the critical path and uncertainty. Update forecasts from evidence rather than preserving an obsolete baseline.
  3. How do you manage budget and forecast? Explain approved baseline, committed/actual cost, remaining forecast, contingency rules and forecast at completion. Investigate variance and present options. State which costs and approvals you actually controlled; do not invent a programme budget.
  4. How do you manage scope change? Describe the request, impact on business value, cost, date, quality and dependencies. Obtain approval at the right level, update the baseline and communicate it. An Agile backlog still needs visibility when changes affect contractual commitments.
  5. What makes RAID useful? Give each significant item an owner, impact, action and review/decision date. Distinguish a risk from an issue, and an assumption from a known dependency. Explain a decision your RAID process enabled.
  6. How do you manage a fixed regulatory deadline? Identify the precise obligation with the relevant specialists, minimum compliant scope and evidence needed. Work backwards through testing and approvals; escalate early with options. Regulatory urgency does not justify silently accepting unsafe release risk.
  7. How do you govern suppliers and stakeholders? Agree deliverables, interfaces, milestones, evidence and escalation rights. Use a short decision paper for sponsor choices. Describe how you managed disagreement without hiding it in a green report.
  8. How do you close and realise benefits? Confirm acceptance, support ownership, outstanding risks, contracts, documentation and lessons. Assign benefits owners and measurement dates beyond project closure. Savings can be forecast, avoided cost or cash released; say which.

Worked infrastructure answer

Use the March 2020–May 2022 Northern Powergrid infrastructure initiative to explain one migration or upgrade decision. The portfolio reports 120+ servers and 30+ applications and approximately £400k annual savings. Describe the dependency, outage consequence, sequencing choice, change preparation, rollback and business validation that you personally coordinated. Explain who approved the release and residual risk.

Do not open with “I managed a £400k programme” unless you separately have a verified budget. Annual savings and delivery budget are different. Explain how the savings estimate was produced and whether realised benefits were tracked after transition.

Governance scenario — illustrative, not historical

A sponsor wants a fixed date, a supplier is two weeks late and the cost forecast is worsening. Validate dependency dates and the remaining critical path. Confirm whether the date is contractual, regulatory or aspirational. Present a short options paper: change scope, sequence differently, add targeted capacity with credible onboarding assumptions, or move the milestone. Include cost, residual risk, affected benefits and approval needed.

Seek a decision before the option expires; do not wait for the next monthly board. Update the plan, forecast and change log after approval. Monitor evidence against the revised milestones. If the sponsor accepts risk, document the actual acceptance and any controls; do not interpret silence as approval.

For an earned-value question, define planned value, earned value and actual cost before using schedule or cost indices. Explain whether the work packages and acceptance evidence support that method. Do not apply a precise index to loosely defined progress percentages or confuse schedule variance with a forecast completion date.

Architect interviews test judgement: clarifying requirements before naming technology, choosing between credible options, explaining trade-offs in business terms and designing for failure. This guide has two tracks. Track A prepares solution and AI architect interviews: client-facing options, cloud and AI architecture, governance and the architecture review board. Your architecture evidence is the decisions you made or shaped in those roles, plus three public projects where you were the architect. Say that once, clearly, then let the reasoning carry the interview. Study depth is in the AI Architect's Handbook, the System Design Concept Handbook and the architecture curriculum.

Track A — solution and AI architecture

Q1. How do you design an AI solution that must run in a sovereign or UK-only cloud environment?

Model answer: "Start from the obligations: which data can leave which boundary, who may administer the platform, and what the customer must be able to audit. Then every component has to comply, not just storage — the model endpoint must be hosted in-region or self-hosted, logs and traces stay in-region, encryption keys are customer-managed, and support access is controlled. That often narrows the model choice, so I'd evaluate the in-region options on the actual workload early, before the design depends on a model that isn't available there." [1]

Q2. Open-source models or proprietary API models for an enterprise?

Model answer: "Not a belief, a comparison on the workload. Proprietary APIs usually lead on capability and speed of adoption; open-weight models give control over data, versioning and cost at high volume, but the organisation then owns GPUs, patching, scaling and evaluation of every upgrade. I compare cost per successful task, data and contractual requirements, latency, and whether the team can operate it. A common answer is both: a hosted model for complex work and a small self-hosted model for high-volume simple tasks, behind one gateway."

Q3. How do you ensure lineage, security and compliance in a distributed AI architecture?

Model answer: "Treat every derived artefact — chunks, embeddings, prompts, caches, fine-tuning sets, logs — as data with an owner, a purpose and a retention rule. Record lineage from source to answer: which documents and versions were retrieved for each response. Enforce access control at retrieval time, encrypt in transit and at rest, keep secrets out of model context, and make deletion propagate through every derived copy. Then prove it with audit logs and periodic tests rather than a policy document." [1]

Q4. How do you manage model drift and keep performance up in production?

Model answer: "Distinguish three things that look alike: the world changing (new kinds of input), our system changing (prompt, index, model version) and the provider changing the model underneath us. Pin versions where possible, run a fixed evaluation set on every change and on a schedule, monitor online signals such as escalation and correction rates by segment, and add new failure cases to the evaluation set. A threshold breach triggers a defined response — rollback, reduced autonomy or retraining — not just an alert."

Q5. Design a question-answering system over internal documentation that stays relevant and fresh.

Model answer: "Clarify users, document sources, permissions and how stale is acceptable. Ingest incrementally using content hashes so changed documents are re-indexed and deleted ones removed; carry access-control metadata onto every chunk and filter before generation. Use hybrid keyword and vector search with reranking, show citations with document dates, and let the system say when no current source answers. Measure retrieval recall and answer faithfulness on questions from real users, and give content owners a report of unanswered questions." [3]

Q6. How would you reduce token costs at scale?

Model answer: "Measure cost per successful task by feature first. Then route simple requests to smaller models, cache stable prompt prefixes and repeated answers, send fewer and shorter retrieved chunks, cap output length, batch asynchronous work and remove retries that loop. Each change has to hold quality on the evaluation set. In ResearchOps I kept scoring on a local model and used the paid model only for the top items, with a hard daily cost ceiling."

Q7. How do you detect and reduce ungrounded outputs?

Model answer: "Ground answers in retrieved sources with citations, check automatically that cited passages exist and support the claim where possible, allow the system to abstain, and measure the unsupported-claim rate on a labelled set. For high-consequence answers add human review. The architecture must make abstention a normal, visible outcome rather than an error."

Q8. Fine-tune a model or use retrieval for a domain-specific assistant?

Model answer: "Retrieval first for knowledge that changes or must be cited — it is cheaper to update and easier to debug. Fine-tuning suits stable behaviour, style or format that prompting can't deliver reliably, or moving a proven task to a smaller model for cost or latency. The decision should come from an evaluation of both on the same cases, not from preference." [3]

Q9. How do you decide between building and buying?

Model answer: "Buy what doesn't differentiate the business; build what does or what no product does well. I compare total cost over several years, including integration and operation, the exit cost if the vendor changes terms, data and security requirements, and how much the capability needs to change with the business. The Smart Timesheet is a useful example: an OutSystems front end on AWS for speed and cost, with a .NET integration layer to the client's Oracle HCM and CRM where control mattered." Say who made that platform decision. [2]

Q10. A stakeholder wants everything multi-region active-active. How do you push back?

Model answer: "Ask what an hour of downtime actually costs for each service and what recovery time and data-loss limits the business needs. Active-active roughly doubles infrastructure and adds data-consistency complexity, so I'd propose it only where the numbers justify it and offer cheaper patterns elsewhere — a warm standby or backup-and-restore — with the cost and recovery time of each side by side. The stakeholder then chooses with the trade-off visible." [2]

Q11. How do you keep a cloud bill from quietly tripling?

Model answer: "Treat cost as a design requirement: tag resources to owners and products, set budgets and anomaly alerts, review cost per transaction or per customer rather than the total, right-size and schedule non-production environments, and use committed-use pricing only for steady load. For AI workloads add token and request dashboards, because model spend grows with prompts and usage without any code change." [2]

Q12. Which well-architected pillar do teams ignore most?

Model answer: "The AWS framework has six pillars: operational excellence, security, reliability, performance efficiency, cost optimisation and sustainability. In my experience operational excellence is the one most neglected — runbooks, observability, safe deployment and learning from incidents — because it doesn't show up in a demo. It's the pillar that decides whether the other five survive the first year in production."

Q13. How does architecture make AI governance real rather than a document?

Model answer: "By turning obligations into enforced controls with evidence: an inventory of AI systems and their risk tier; approval gates in the deployment pipeline; access control and logging at the model gateway; evaluation results attached to every release; and a way to reduce or withdraw a system's authority quickly. Frameworks such as NIST's AI Risk Management Framework and ISO/IEC 42001 help structure this. In the EU, Article 50 transparency duties have applied since August 2026 and the AI Omnibus moved most high-risk obligations to December 2027, so the timeline must be checked for each system rather than assumed."

Track B — software architecture and system design

Q14. Design a system for something we actually do. What are your first three decisions?

Model answer: "First, what the system must guarantee — the one or two non-functional requirements that dominate, such as consistency for payments or latency for search. Second, the boundaries: which parts change together and which teams own them, which shapes services and data ownership. Third, the data model and where the source of truth lives. Technology choices come after those, and I'd say what would make me change each decision." [2]

Q15. When would you choose a monolith over microservices?

Model answer: "Usually at the start, and whenever the team is small or the domain boundaries are still unclear. A well-structured modular monolith is faster to change and simpler to operate. I'd split a service out when there's a clear reason — independent scaling, a different release cadence, a separate team, or isolation of a risky component — and accept the cost in networking, observability and data consistency." [2]

Q16. Strong or eventual consistency — how do you decide, and how do you keep data correct across services?

Model answer: "By the cost of a wrong or stale read. Balances and inventory reservations need strong consistency at the point of decision; a product catalogue or a feed can be eventually consistent. Across services I avoid distributed transactions: the transactional outbox pattern publishes events reliably with the local change, consumers are idempotent, and a saga with compensating actions handles multi-step business processes."

Q17. How would you scale a read-heavy service?

Model answer: "Measure first. Then cache at the right layer — CDN for static content, an application cache for hot objects — with an explicit invalidation strategy, add read replicas and accept their replication lag where the use case allows, and denormalise or precompute views for expensive queries. Watch the cache hit rate and the load on the database when the cache is cold."

Q18. Design a distributed job queue with priorities, retries and a dead-letter queue.

Model answer: "Producers write jobs with an ID, priority and idempotency key to a durable store or broker. Workers pull by priority with a visibility timeout, so a crashed worker's job reappears. Failures retry with exponential backoff and jitter up to a limit, then move to a dead-letter queue with the error for inspection and replay. Jobs must be idempotent because delivery is at least once. Expose queue depth, age of the oldest job and retry rates, and protect low-priority work from starvation." [4]

Q19. Design a rate limiter with per-user and global limits.

Model answer: "A token bucket per user and one global bucket, both checked atomically on each request — in a shared store such as Redis with a script, so multiple API instances agree. Return a clear 429 with a retry-after time. Decide what happens if the store is unavailable — fail open for low-risk endpoints, closed for expensive ones — and monitor rejection rates so legitimate users aren't being throttled." [4]

Q20. How do you design APIs that others can depend on?

Model answer: "Contract first: clear resources, consistent errors, pagination, idempotency keys for writes, and versioning with a deprecation policy. Design for the consumer's workflow rather than the database tables, document with examples, and test the contract automatically so a change that breaks consumers fails the build."

Q21. How would you modernise a legacy estate without stopping the business?

Model answer: "Inventory and classify first — retire, upgrade, migrate or replace — then sequence by business risk and dependency, not by technology. Use the strangler-fig approach for applications: route traffic through a façade and move capabilities one at a time. Every change needs rollback and production validation agreed with the application owner. At Northern Powergrid we treated 120-plus servers and 30-plus applications differently by category rather than as one migration, which kept production stable."

Q22. How do you design for multiple tenants?

Model answer: "Choose the isolation model per layer — shared, pooled with tenant IDs, or dedicated — based on security, noisy-neighbour risk and cost, and enforce tenant identity at every query, cache key, log and backup. When we adapted Natoora's internal platform for external customers, the decisions were about white-labelling and configuration without forking the product, plus a repeatable onboarding process, so a second customer did not mean a second codebase."

Q23. How do you define and use SLOs?

Model answer: "Pick a few indicators users feel — availability, latency, correctness — set objectives with the business, and use the error budget to balance release speed and reliability: if the budget is spent, the team prioritises reliability work. For AI features add behavioural indicators such as unsupported-answer rate, because a fast, available service can still be wrong."

Influence and judgement

Q24. Tell me about a design you got wrong.

Model answer: "My first version of ResearchOps used CrewAI with hard-coded sources. It worked for AI topics but I'd designed the topic and the source list into the code, so every new subject meant code changes. I wrote proper requirements for version two — any topic as a configuration file, sources discovered and vetted at run time, ranking that can be audited, and a cost ceiling — and rebuilt it rather than patching. The lesson I apply now: separate what changes often from what doesn't, and write that down before choosing a framework." [2]

Q25. You designed the right solution and the team didn't want to build it. What did you do?

Model answer: "Find out why before escalating — resistance often contains information. When I wanted Natoora to move from Trello to Jira, I documented the problems for two weeks, compared four tools on workflow, reporting, integrations and cost, and asked for a free, reversible pilot on one initiative. I designed the workflows around how the team actually worked and came back with the pilot results. Approval followed the evidence; I hadn't needed to win an argument." [2]

Q26. Explain a hard technical trade-off as if I were the CFO.

Model answer: "Put it in money, time and risk. For example: 'A full white-label would take six to eight weeks; the client needs four. If we brand the customer-facing screens, emails, login and reports first and defer internal admin screens, we meet the date. The risk is that internal users work with unbranded admin screens for a while, which costs us nothing with the customer.' No jargon, one recommendation, and what would change it."

Q27. Sales promised a client something the architecture can't do. What happens next week?

Model answer: "Separate what was promised from what is buildable, quickly and honestly. I'd find the outcome the client actually needs, propose what we can deliver and when, with the cost of the alternative, and go with the account lead to the client together rather than leaving sales to backtrack alone. Then fix the process: a technical review before commitments of that kind." [2]

Q28. When is "good enough" the right architecture?

Model answer: "When it meets the requirement with a clear path to change, and the cost of more design exceeds the risk it removes. IncidentPilot's first version is deliberately read-only: it can't restart anything. That made it safe enough to run and evaluate now, and the evaluation will tell me when approval-gated actions are justified."

Q29. What do you document, and what do you let the code carry?

Model answer: "Document the why: decisions, alternatives rejected and the conditions that would reopen them, in short architecture decision records next to the code. Let the code, tests and API contracts carry the what. A diagram that nobody updates is worse than none, so I keep a small number of current views." [2]

Q30. Tell me about mentoring someone through a hard technical problem.

Model answer: Use the mentoring record in B3: at Natoora you coached engineers on test-first development, pair programming and DevOps practices. Prepare one specific person, the problem, how you helped them reason rather than handing over the answer, and what they then did on their own.

Presenting an architecture

If asked to present, tell a story: the problem and who has it, the requirements that drive the design, two or three options with their trade-offs, the decision, how it fails and how you'd know, the cost, and what you'd do next. Leave time for questions; the defence matters more than the slides.

Credibility probes

Were you the architect or the delivery lead? Who signed off the design? What did it cost to run? What broke in production? Which decision would you reverse? For the personal projects: what load have they handled, and what would you change before a customer used them? Answer within your actual role; reasoning quality earns the credit.

Questions to ask the panel

How are architecture decisions made and recorded here? How much of the role is hands-on? Which non-functional requirement causes the most trouble today? How do you govern AI use across teams?

Panels reward a working system with numbers more than definitions. Your position: a delivery and product leader who now builds hands-on, with one production AI rollout and three public projects. Fundamentals

Model answers are written in your voice from the evidence bank. Where one describes a habit rather than a recorded fact, keep only what you actually do.

Q1. A user asks a question about a 200-page PDF. What happens between enter and the answer?

Model answer: "Two phases. Offline, I parse the PDF keeping its structure, split it into chunks along sections with a little overlap, embed each chunk and also index it for keyword search, storing page and section metadata. At query time I may rewrite the question, run hybrid retrieval, rerank the top candidates and pass only the best few chunks, with IDs, into a prompt that tells the model to answer from them and cite them. I check the citations point at real chunks before returning, and I stream the answer. Most latency is the generated tokens, not reading the prompt. If the question needs a live number, such as an account balance, retrieval is the wrong tool and I call an API instead."

Q2. Is a bigger context window always better?

Model answer: "No. A longer prompt costs more, takes longer to process, and models use information in the middle of long contexts less reliably. I retrieve less but better, put the most relevant evidence first, and measure answer quality as I change top-k rather than assuming more context helps." [4]

Q3. What does temperature change, and when do you set it to zero?

Model answer: "It reshapes the probability distribution the next token is sampled from: higher spreads it out, lower sharpens it. I use low or zero for extraction and tool calls, but zero is not a determinism guarantee, so I still validate output against a schema and retry on failure. Reliability comes from schemas, validators and evaluation, not from the sampling setting."

Q4. Why is generating the answer slower than reading the prompt?

Model answer: "The prompt is processed in parallel in one prefill pass; the output is produced one token at a time, each step reading the cached keys and values of everything before it. So time to first token mostly tracks prompt length, and total time mostly tracks output length. That is why shorter outputs, streaming and prefix caching matter."

Q5. When would you fine-tune instead of improving the prompt or adding retrieval?

Model answer: "Last, and only with evidence. I start with prompt structure and examples, measured on an evaluation set. If the gap is knowledge that changes or must be cited, that is retrieval. Fine-tuning fits stable behaviour the prompt cannot reliably produce — a strict format, a narrow classification — or moving a proven task to a smaller, cheaper model. It adds a data pipeline, versioning and a rollback path, so it has to beat the cheaper options on the same evaluation set."

Q6. Your embedding search returns results that look related but are not what the user meant. What is happening?

Model answer: "Similarity is not relevance. Embeddings capture topic, so a question about cancelling an order can match an article about placing one, and they are weak on exact identifiers like error codes. I'd measure recall on a small labelled set first, then try hybrid search with keyword matching, a reranker, metadata filters and better chunk boundaries before paying to swap the embedding model."

Retrieval

Q7. How do you choose a chunk size?

Model answer: "From the documents and the questions, not a default. Small chunks are precise but lose context; large ones dilute the signal and cost tokens. I split on document structure — headings, sections, table rows — test two or three sizes against retrieval recall and answer quality on real questions, and keep parent-section metadata so I can expand context when needed."

Q8. Why hybrid search and a reranker?

Model answer: "Keyword search catches exact terms and identifiers; vector search catches paraphrases. Combining them, usually with reciprocal rank fusion, improves recall. A cross-encoder reranker then scores each query–passage pair more accurately but is too slow for the whole corpus, so I retrieve broadly, rerank, and keep the top few."

Q9. What happens when the documents do not answer the question?

Model answer: "The system should say so. I set a relevance threshold, tell the model it may answer 'I don't know', and log the unanswered question so the content owner can fill the gap. In evaluation I include unanswerable questions and score a confident wrong answer as worse than an honest abstention."

Q10. How do you keep the index fresh and permission-safe?

Model answer: "Incremental ingestion keyed on content hashes, so changed documents are re-embedded and deleted ones are removed — deletes are the step teams forget. I store access-control metadata on every chunk and filter before anything reaches the model; the prompt is never the permission boundary. If I change embedding model, I re-embed everything and re-run the retrieval evaluation."

Q11. How does agentic RAG differ from a single retrieval pass?

Model answer: "The model decides whether the evidence is enough and, if not, searches again with a better query, which helps multi-part questions. It costs latency, tokens and loop logic, so I cap iterations and keep it only for the question types where evaluation shows it beats a fixed pipeline."

Agents and tools

Q12. How does tool calling actually work?

Model answer: "I describe each tool with a schema. The model returns a structured request — a tool name and arguments — instead of prose. My code validates the arguments, checks permissions, runs the tool and appends the result to the conversation. The model never executes anything itself; every safety control lives in that code."

Q13. When would you not use an agent?

Model answer: "When the steps are known in advance, a fixed workflow is cheaper, faster and easier to test. In IncidentPilot I used deterministic rules for the diagnosis that can be known, and gave the model one narrow job — ranking hypotheses from validated evidence — with no tools at all. If the model times out, the rules-only diagnosis still works."

Q14. An agent loops forever on some inputs. How do you find and fix it?

Model answer: "I open the trace for a failing run and find the step that repeats. Common causes are a tool error the agent cannot see because it was swallowed, an ambiguous tool result, or no stop condition. I fix the cause — return a structured error, tighten the tool description, add an explicit 'done' state — and add guards: a maximum number of steps and tokens, a timeout, detection of the same call with the same arguments, and a partial result instead of silence. Then I replay the failing inputs as fixed test cases before shipping."

Q15. How do you make tools safe to give an agent?

Model answer: "Least privilege, an allow-list of actions, arguments validated on the server, idempotency keys on anything that writes, and human confirmation for anything irreversible or expensive. Hatch is a good example: it prepares CV packs and tracks applications, but it never submits an application — a person always does that step."

Q16. What is MCP, and how is it different from function calling?

Model answer: "Function calling is a model API feature: your application describes tools and handles the calls. MCP is an open protocol for exposing tools, resources and prompts from a server so that any compatible host can use them without a bespoke integration. The current specification, 2026-07-28, makes requests stateless and adds server discovery. MCP standardises the connection; it does not decide who may call a tool or whether a call is safe — authorisation and approval remain my job." [5]

Q17. How do you build an agent that runs for days?

Model answer: "Keep state outside the conversation: a durable store with checkpoints, so a crash or restart resumes rather than repeats. Make external actions idempotent, because a checkpoint does not prove a side effect did not already happen. Hatch uses LangGraph with a SQLite checkpointer for this. Add human approval points and an expiry for stale approvals."

Q18. When are multiple agents worth it?

Model answer: "When the work splits into parts that genuinely need different instructions, tools or models, or an independent check. In Hatch, Scout, Scorer, Tailor and Coach do different jobs with different context, and a human review sits between tailoring and applying. If one agent with good tools meets the target, more agents just add coordination cost and new failure modes."

Evaluation

Q19. How do you know your AI feature is good — not your gut, your number?

Model answer: "I define the task and what good means with the people who own it, build a labelled evaluation set from real cases including hard and unanswerable ones, and grade with deterministic checks first and a calibrated judge only where meaning needs interpretation. I set pass thresholds from the cost of failure and look at results by slice, not just the average. In IncidentPilot an evaluation passes only if all ten checks pass, including that the right cause ranked first and that no action was executed."

Q20. How do you know a prompt change made the whole product better, not just one example?

Model answer: "Run the versioned evaluation set before and after, compare per-slice results and inspect the cases that changed, then gate the change in CI so a regression blocks the merge. After release I watch online signals such as escalations and user corrections. One improved example is an anecdote."

Q21. When do you trust an LLM as a judge?

Model answer: "After checking it. I label a representative sample myself or with a domain expert, measure agreement, look at where the judge and humans disagree, and re-check after any model or rubric change. I use deterministic checks — schema, exact match, citation exists — wherever they can answer the question, because they do not drift."

Q22. How do you evaluate a RAG system?

Model answer: "Separately for retrieval and generation, so I know which layer failed. Retrieval: recall@k and precision on labelled question–passage pairs. Generation: faithfulness to the retrieved text, answer relevance and citation validity. End to end: task success on real questions, including ones the corpus cannot answer."

Q23. How do you evaluate an agent?

Model answer: "Task success on fixed scenarios with known end states, checked by inspecting the resulting state rather than the agent's description of it. Then the trajectory — tool choice, wasted steps, loops — and cost per task. Agents vary run to run, so I repeat trials and report how often every attempt succeeds, not just the best run."

Q24. A customer says the bot feels dumb. What do you change first?

Model answer: "I'd collect twenty or thirty of the conversations they mean and sort the failures — wrong answer, refusal, slow, missing context, tone. Then I'd find the first failing layer: often retrieval missing the right document, not the model. I fix the cheapest layer that explains most failures, add those cases to the evaluation set and agree a measure with the customer so 'better' is visible."

Production, cost and reliability

Q25. The token bill tripled last month and nobody changed the code. What happened?

Model answer: "Something changed outside the code. I'd check traffic, then tokens per request: retrieval returning more or longer chunks, conversation history growing, retries or agent loops, a lower prompt-cache hit rate because the prompt prefix changed, or a provider model or price change. A per-request cost breakdown by feature answers this in minutes; without one it takes days."

Q26. Your feature is too slow and too expensive. What do you do?

Model answer: "Measure where time and tokens go first. Then route easy requests to a smaller model, cache repeated prefixes and answers, send fewer chunks, cap output length, stream, and run independent tool calls in parallel — keeping each change only if quality holds on the evaluation set. ResearchOps shows the pattern: local scoring, the paid model only for the top items, and a hard daily cost ceiling."

Q27. What do you log for an LLM feature?

Model answer: "Request and prompt-template version, model and parameters, retrieved document IDs, tool calls and results, the output, latency split into time to first token and total, tokens, cost, and any evaluation or user-feedback signal — linked by one trace ID. I minimise sensitive content: Hatch's optional traces deliberately exclude prompts, responses and CV text."

Q28. How do you reduce hallucinations?

Model answer: "In layers: ground answers in retrieved evidence with citations, allow and reward abstention, constrain the output format, verify claims or citations automatically where possible, and measure the unsupported-claim rate on an evaluation set so I know whether a change helped."

Q29. Your model provider has an outage. What happens?

Model answer: "A tested fallback, not any model that accepts the same request — a fallback changes behaviour, so it must pass the same evaluation. Where no fallback is safe, the system degrades gracefully. IncidentPilot falls back to rules-only diagnosis; it never silently returns a worse answer as if nothing happened."

Q30. Local or hosted models?

Model answer: "It depends on data sensitivity, volume, latency and who will operate it. Hosted models are faster to start and stronger; local models keep data on the machine and cost nothing per call but bring hardware, updates and monitoring. Hatch supports both: a small local triage model filters roles quickly and a larger one does detailed work, or a cloud provider handles both."

Security and safety

Q31. How do you defend an agent against prompt injection?

Model answer: "Assume it will happen, because there is no complete fix. Treat retrieved text and tool results as data, keep secrets out of the model's context, give tools least privilege, validate actions outside the model, and require approval for anything consequential. The strongest control is limiting what injected text could make the system do — in IncidentPilot the model has no tools at all."

Q32. How do you handle personal data in an LLM application?

Model answer: "Minimise what reaches the model, redact where possible, choose a provider and region that meet the data requirements, set retention for logs and traces, and support deletion across derived copies such as embeddings and caches."

Projects and behavioural

Q33. Walk me through a project you built end to end.

Model answer (STAR): "Situation: I wanted a triage assistant for containerised services, and to test how far an LLM should be trusted in that loop. Task: Build a triage agent that is useful and safe enough to run on real services. Action: I made the MVP read-only. A collector gathers runtime status, bounded logs, health checks and deployment data; deterministic rules diagnose the known failure patterns; an optional local model ranks hypotheses from validated evidence and has no tools; rules keep control of severity and recommended actions. I built golden-file evaluations for two injected failures with ten checks each, including 'no action executed', and a CI job that injects both failures into a live demo stack. Result: It produces JSON and SRE-style reports with or without the model, and every change has to keep both scenarios passing. Next: more scenarios, and approval-gated actions only once evaluation supports them."

Q34. Tell me about a time your evaluation said no.

Model answer: "In July I ran a staged benchmark to see whether a newer local model should replace Hatch's writing baseline. All 27 runs completed with valid output and no unsupported claims, but no challenger passed the hard quality gates, so I kept the baseline. The point of the gates is that a newer model does not ship just because it is newer."

Q35. Why did you rebuild ResearchOps rather than extend it?

Model answer: "Version one used CrewAI with hard-coded sources, so it only worked for AI topics. I wrote requirements for version two: any topic as a file, sources discovered and vetted at run time, ranking I could audit, no hard-coded credentials and a cost ceiling. Four of five ranking scores are now deterministic and only relevance is model-judged. Extending v1 would have kept its narrow design."

Q36. How do you use coding agents in your own work?

Model answer: "As fast contributors working from a specification I write. I give them bounded context and a test that must pass, review every diff and keep decisions in the repository rather than chat history. I've used IncidentPilot as a benchmark project to compare Claude Code, Codex, OpenCode and Cline on the same tasks." Add one finding from that comparison that you can defend.

Q37. You've been a delivery lead. Why AI engineering, and what is your gap?

Model answer: "I've spent years making sure systems create value in production, and the hardest problems in AI products now are exactly that: evaluation, safe integration, rollout and cost. I've backed that with hands-on work. My honest gap is operating an AI system at high scale as the engineer on call; I've built the evaluation, tracing and fallback patterns that scale needs, and I'd expect to close that gap quickly in a team that already runs at scale."

Practice exercises

Build in 40 minutes: a FastAPI endpoint that answers questions over a folder of Markdown files with keyword plus vector retrieval, cites chunk IDs and abstains below a threshold. Get it working end to end before optimising. [3]

Take-home rehearsal: 50 documents, 20 questions with labelled answers including three unanswerable ones. Report recall@5, faithfulness and abstention accuracy, and one change you made because of the numbers.

Debugging rehearsal: an agent that repeats a tool call. Find the swallowed error, add a step cap and a repeated-call guard, and replay the failing input as a test.

Credibility probes

Which parts did you write and which did a coding agent write? How was the 90% order-automation figure measured? What does your evaluation set not cover? What would break first at a thousand users? Have you been on call for an AI system? Answer each directly; "personal project" is a respectable answer when it is true.

Questions to ask the panel

How do you evaluate model or prompt changes before release? Who owns an AI feature's quality after launch? What does your observability show for an LLM call today? Which failure in production taught the team the most?

Decomposition

Use one sequence and say it out loud: clarify the user, the outcome and how success is measured → map inputs, owners and freshness → constraints (security, regulation, budget, timeline) → workstreams → a walking skeleton that proves the path end to end in weeks → risks and failure modes → measure and iterate. Ask before solving; label assumptions; sequence by risk. [1][3]

Q1. An insurer wants LLM-powered summaries across 30 million historical claims. Scope the first 90 days.

Model answer: "First, who reads the summaries and what decision do they support — a handler picking up a claim, fraud review, or a regulator? That decides what 'good' means. I'd ask how claims are stored, which documents are scanned, what personal and special-category data they hold, where processing is allowed, and what a handler does today and how long it takes. Then I'd propose a thin slice: one claim type, a few hundred historical claims, a summary format agreed with handlers, and an evaluation set they label — factual accuracy against source documents, missing critical facts, and time saved. Weeks one to four: data access, a secure processing path and the baseline. Weeks five to eight: pilot with ten handlers on live claims, summaries shown alongside the originals, never replacing them. Weeks nine to twelve: decide on scale from the numbers. I would not batch-process all 30 million until the quality, cost per claim and data-protection position are proven; at that volume a cost per summary that is a few pence too high is a real budget line."

Q2. How would you help a wheelchair user get through a busy train station on their own?

Model answer: "I'd start with the person, not the technology: which part of the journey fails today — finding step-free routes, lifts out of service, boarding assistance not arriving, crowds? I'd want to shadow a few journeys. Data sources would include station maps, live lift status, platform changes and the assistance-booking system, each with an owner and a freshness problem; a lift status that is ten minutes stale is worse than none. A first version might be live step-free routing with lift status and a one-tap 'I need help at this point' that reaches staff. Success is a measure the user cares about — journeys completed without unplanned help — and the failure mode to design against is confident wrong directions." [3]

Q3. A city wants to reduce emergency response times. Where do you start?

Model answer: "Break response time into its parts — call answering, triage, dispatch, travel and handover — and get data on each before assuming where the delay is. The fix for slow call answering is staffing or routing, not a model; for travel time it may be station placement. I'd pick the stage with the biggest measured delay and the fewest safety risks, ship a decision aid for dispatchers rather than an automated decision, and measure before and after."

Customer conversations

Graders look for ownership language, diagnostic questions before solutions, honest options with trade-offs, and no promises you cannot keep. [1]

Q4. The deployment has slipped three weeks and the customer's CTO is on the call.

Model answer: "I'd open with the facts and own them: what slipped, why, and what it affects for them. Then options, not excuses — for example go live on time with a reduced scope that still covers the main workflow, keep the full scope with a new date backed by a revised plan, or add specific capacity with its cost. I'd recommend one, say what I need from them to hit it, and agree when they'll next hear from me. When this happened with a SaaS client expecting go-live in four weeks against our six-to-eight-week estimate, an impact matrix let us agree which screens went first; we went live on schedule with fewer than ten issues in a 30-day pilot."

Q5. The customer wants a feature that would break data-governance rules.

Model answer: "I'd find the outcome behind the request — usually speed or visibility — and say clearly that I can't build it the way it was asked and why, in their terms: the regulatory or reputational exposure, not 'policy says no'. Then I'd offer a compliant route to the same outcome, bring their data-protection or security owner into the decision, and record it. Holding the line once, politely and with an alternative, builds more trust than quietly building something that later has to be pulled."

Q6. Explain to a non-technical VP why the assistant can't guarantee 100% accuracy.

Model answer: "The assistant predicts likely answers from the documents it finds; it doesn't look facts up like a database. So, like a well-briefed new colleague, it is usually right and occasionally wrong. What we can guarantee is how we manage that: it shows its sources, says 'I don't know' when evidence is missing, we measure its error rate on real questions before launch, and anything consequential gets a human check. The useful question is which error rate is acceptable for this task and what it costs compared with today."

Q7. The customer's security team won't give you production credentials.

Model answer: "They're right to be careful, so I'd make their job easy rather than escalate. I'd ask what they need to approve access — a threat model, least-privilege scopes, audit logging, a named service identity — and offer a staged path: synthetic or masked data first, read-only access next, write access only for specific actions with approval. Meanwhile I'd build against a realistic test environment so the project keeps moving."

Q8. You disagree with the architecture the customer has chosen.

Model answer: "I'd first make sure I understand why they chose it — there's often a constraint I can't see. If I still disagree, I'd put the concern in terms of their outcomes: cost, risk or time, with evidence, and propose the smallest test that would settle it. If they still choose their option, I'd document the risk, agree how we'd notice it, and commit to making their choice work. It is their system; my job is to make sure the decision is informed."

Q9. The customer says the model is getting worse.

Model answer: "I'd ask for examples and dates, then check what changed: the inputs (new document types, a new user group), the retrieval index, the prompt or model version, and upstream data. I'd re-run the original evaluation set — if scores hold, the world has moved and the evaluation set needs new cases; if they dropped, something in our system changed and the traces will show where. I'd report back with evidence either way."

Technical and system design

Q10. Design a private, VPC-deployed RAG system for a healthcare customer with 50 million documents.

Model answer: "Clarify users, question types, latency, and the regulatory frame — in the UK that means UK GDPR, the NHS Data Security and Protection Toolkit and data-residency requirements. Everything runs inside their cloud account: ingestion workers that parse and chunk documents with access-control metadata, a vector and keyword index, a model either self-hosted or reached through a private endpoint in the right region, and an API behind their single sign-on. Permissions are filtered at retrieval time, before anything reaches the model. Audit logs record who asked what and which documents were used, with retention agreed with their information-governance team. At 50 million documents, incremental ingestion and re-indexing cost matter as much as query latency. I'd prove the path with one department's documents and an evaluation set clinicians help label, then scale."

Q11. Build an evaluation harness for a shipment-rerouting agent with a 99% on-time target.

Model answer: "Separate the agent's decisions from the business outcome. Build scenarios from historical disruptions with known good reroutes, and grade the end state in a sandbox — was the shipment rerouted, within cost limits, without breaking a constraint — rather than trusting the agent's description. Add policy checks that must always pass, repeat each scenario several times because agents vary, and track cost per decision. In production, shadow the agent against planners before letting it act, and watch on-time rate by lane and carrier."

Q12. How would you diagnose high latency in an LLM pipeline?

Model answer: "Trace one slow request end to end and split the time: network and queueing, retrieval, time to first token, generation, and tool calls. Long prompts raise time to first token; long outputs raise generation time; sequential tool calls add up. Fixes follow the finding — smaller or cached prompts, shorter outputs, streaming, parallel calls, a smaller model for simple requests, or more serving capacity if requests are queueing."

Q13. Make an LLM-powered search go from 1.5 seconds to 100 milliseconds.

Model answer: "At 100 milliseconds a generative call is probably not on the critical path. I'd keep retrieval and ranking fast — precomputed embeddings, an approximate-nearest-neighbour index, caching of popular queries — and move generation off the hot path: show results instantly and stream a summary afterwards, or precompute summaries for common queries. I'd confirm with the customer which part must be 100 milliseconds."

Q14. RAG, fine-tuning or prompting — how do you choose for a customer?

Model answer: "Prompting and examples first, measured on the customer's own evaluation set. Retrieval when the knowledge changes or must be cited. Fine-tuning only for stable behaviour or format the prompt cannot deliver, or to move a proven task to a smaller model — and only after the cheaper options have been tested on the same set."

Q15. Hosted API or self-hosted open-weight model for an enterprise customer?

Model answer: "Data residency and contractual terms often decide it. Hosted models are stronger and faster to adopt; self-hosting gives control over data and cost at high volume but brings GPUs, patching, monitoring and capacity planning. I'd compare total cost per successful task, the customer's ability to operate it, and the evaluation results of both on their workload."

Q16. A third-party API the customer depends on times out intermittently.

Model answer: "Set timeouts, retry with exponential backoff and jitter, and only retry idempotent calls or use idempotency keys. Add a circuit breaker so a failing dependency doesn't take everything down, a fallback or queued retry for the user, and alerting with the evidence the supplier will need. Then share the data with the supplier rather than absorbing the problem silently."

Q17. It's 2 a.m. and your deployment in the customer's environment is down.

Model answer: "Contain first: restore service — roll back, switch to the fallback, or disable the failing feature — and tell the customer's on-call contact what is happening. Preserve logs and traces. Once stable, find the cause, run a blameless post-incident review with the customer, and turn the cause into a test or alert so it can't recur silently."

Q18. How do you protect a customer-facing agent from prompt injection?

Model answer: "Assume injected instructions will get in, because filters miss some. Limit what they can achieve: least-privilege tools, no secrets in context, validation of every action outside the model, and approval for anything consequential. In IncidentPilot I went further — the model has no tools at all and only ranks evidence; deterministic rules decide what is recommended."

Coding practice

FDE coding is practical rather than puzzle-heavy, often in Python, sometimes extended level by level. Practise these until you can narrate while coding: [1][2]

  • A rate limiter with per-user and global limits (token bucket; explain the data structure and concurrency).
  • Parse a messy CSV with inconsistent quoting into clean records, with tests for the edge cases.
  • Exponential backoff with jitter for a flaky API.
  • A streaming consumer that handles backpressure.
  • A small RAG pipeline over a folder of documents, justifying the chunking.
  • SQL: customers whose return rate exceeded 30% last quarter.
  • Reading an unfamiliar 250-line codebase and fixing its bugs: read first, trace the one path that matters, then change it.

Behavioural and values

Q19. Tell me about a real failure.

Model answer: "Earlier in my consulting career I was focused on delivering feature after feature and didn't track usage after release. A client that had invested heavily in one feature came back because almost nobody used it. I interviewed the teams and gathered customer feedback: it had been built exactly to specification, but without real users in mind, so people couldn't find it or use it meaningfully. I recommended a UX review and redesign. What I changed in myself: I now involve users before building and measure adoption after release. That's why the Smart Timesheet went through depot research and a 50-engineer alpha before anything scaled."

Q20. Tell me about spotting a pattern across customers that changed how the team worked.

Model answer: "After the first SaaS client at Natoora, I noticed most of our onboarding pain came from questions we hadn't asked before signing. For the second client I created a structured onboarding pack — a 30-question pre-sales questionnaire, a financial health check, a technical requirements form and a capabilities document. The second implementation took about half the elapsed time of the first, though the scopes weren't identical, so I present that as an observation rather than proof."

Q21. The most technically challenging thing you've owned end to end.

Model answer: Use IncidentPilot from the AI Engineer guide: read-only by design, rules in control, model with no tools, and golden evaluations that inject real failures in CI. Be explicit that it is a personal project and what you would add before running it for a customer.

Q22. Tell me about delivering bad news to a customer.

Model answer: Use the four-week go-live in S2: the honest estimate was six to eight weeks, so you told the client early, showed the impact matrix and agreed which internal screens to defer. Bad news delivered early with options is a negotiation; delivered late, it is a crisis.

Q23. Why this company?

Model answer: Name two or three specific, recent facts — a product, a customer story, a published principle — and connect each to something you have done. For AI labs, read their published charter or safety policies first; generic enthusiasm scores poorly. [2][3]

3. Cost and commercial answers

Panels for consulting roles want proof that you think in money. A Product Owner's commercial job is to decide where the client's budget goes and to show what it returned. Learn this structure and use it in any “business case”, “cost” or “benefits” question.

The five-line cost case (CBMR):

  1. Cost: what it took — people, time, platform, and the opportunity cost of what we did not build.
  2. Benefit: what changes for the business — hours released, cost avoided, revenue, risk reduced.
  3. Measure: baseline, the same measure after release, the period, and who owns the number.
  4. Risk: what could stop the benefit landing — adoption, data quality, support load.
  5. Decision: go, phase, pause or stop, and who approves.

4. Scenario method

Use the same five steps for every “what would you do if…” question. Say the steps out loud. The panel is scoring structure as much as content.

  1. Clarify (20–30 seconds): restate the problem; ask or state two assumptions — the goal, the deadline, who decides.
  2. Options (30 seconds): give three real options with their trade-offs. Doing nothing is often one of them.
  3. Recommend (30 seconds): pick one and say why, linked to the goal. Say who approves it if it is above your authority.
  4. Execute (30–45 seconds): your first concrete steps, in order, with a time box.
  5. Measure (15–30 seconds): how you will know it worked, and when you will review it.

Close by linking it to a real story: “I did something similar at Natoora when…”. That turns a hypothetical into evidence. Six practice scenarios with model answers are in section 6.

6. Scenario drills

These are practice cases, not events from your history. Answer each with the five steps from section 4, then link to a real story.

SC1 — The client PO is unavailable and two stakeholders disagree on launch scope.

  • Clarify: what is the launch date for, who has the authority, and what is in the contract?
  • Options: wait for the client PO; decide alone; or prepare a recommendation and keep working on what both sides agree on.
  • Recommend: the third. Continue reversible, agreed work; send the client PO a one-page decision note with two options and my recommendation; set a deadline for their decision.
  • Execute: today, the decision note and a call with both stakeholders; tomorrow, escalate through the account lead if there is still no decision.
  • Measure: decision made by the deadline; no rework. Agree a named deputy decision-maker so it doesn't happen again.
  • Link: B1 — options shared in advance, escalation route ready.

SC2 — A senior stakeholder demands a new feature halfway through a sprint.

  • Clarify: is it a legal or contractual obligation, what happens if it waits, and does it threaten the sprint goal?
  • Options: put it at the top of the next sprint; swap it for work of equal size if the team agrees and the goal survives; or cancel the sprint if the goal is now obsolete (a PO decision in Scrum).
  • Recommend: usually the next sprint, with the cost of delay made visible. If it is genuinely urgent, swap it in with the developers' agreement.
  • Execute: talk to the stakeholder the same day. Show what would be displaced.
  • Measure: the sprint goal is still met; the stakeholder knows the delivery date.
  • Trap: quietly adding the work, or refusing all change because “the sprint has started”.

SC3 — A service launched on time, but adoption is low.

  • Clarify: compare with the baseline; check which users are dropping out and where in the journey.
  • Options: fix the main barrier; add assisted-digital support; or build the new feature the client is asking for.
  • Recommend: find and fix the main barrier first, using analytics plus research by UCD and the BA with real users.
  • Execute: a two-week investigation, then a small fix released and tested.
  • Measure: completion rate and support contacts against the baseline after four weeks.
  • Link: B2 — the feature nobody used.

SC4 — An AI pilot saves time but sometimes makes serious mistakes. The sponsor wants to scale it.

  • Clarify: how often does it fail, how serious is each failure, and how much time does fixing it take?
  • Options: scale now; scale only the low-risk cases; or pause until validation improves.
  • Recommend: scale the low-risk cases with a human checking anything consequential. Agree release criteria with operations and security.
  • Execute: add validation rules and an exception queue with a named owner; monitor weekly.
  • Measure: net time saved after review effort, error severity and exception volume.
  • Link: S1 — human-in-the-loop, pilot in two regions before expanding.

SC5 — The client cuts the budget by 25% mid-programme. (This tests cost thinking.)

  • Clarify: is the end date fixed, which outcomes matter most, and what is already contracted?
  • Options: cut scope; slow the team down; or change the team mix.
  • Recommend: protect the highest-value outcome and cut low-value scope. Re-order the backlog by cost of delay and show the client the value each option keeps.
  • Execute: within a week, give the client a revised roadmap with two options and their benefit impact; agree the change formally.
  • Measure: the core benefit is still delivered; spend is within the new budget.
  • Link: S2 — four weeks for six to eight weeks of work; deferred internal screens.

SC6 — You inherit a 400-item backlog, the team is six weeks behind and stakeholders are frustrated.

  • Clarify: what is the product goal, what was promised, and why is the team behind?
  • Options: work harder through the list; re-plan the full scope; or reset around a smaller, credible goal.
  • Recommend: reset. In week 1, listen to the team and stakeholders. In week 2, agree the product goal and the top 20 items, archive the rest visibly, and re-baseline the date honestly with the client.
  • Execute: weekly stakeholder review; a Definition of Ready for new items.
  • Measure: sprint goals met for three sprints in a row; stakeholder confidence restored.
  • Link: S7 — Natoora operating model.

Scrum and product ownership

What is the Product Owner accountable for?

“Maximising the value of the product. In practice that means owning the product goal, ordering the backlog and making sure everyone understands it. I can delegate backlog work to a BA, but the accountability stays with me.”

What does the PO do in each Scrum event?

“In sprint planning I bring the product goal and the most valuable items, and we agree the sprint goal together; the developers decide how much they can take on. In refinement I clarify items with the BA, designers and developers. At the sprint review I show the increment to stakeholders and we adapt the backlog. I join the retrospective as a team member.”

Definition of Ready and Definition of Done?

“Done is in the Scrum Guide: the quality standard every increment must meet — for example tested, accessible and deployable. Ready isn't in the Scrum Guide; it's a team agreement that an item is clear enough to start. I use it lightly, so it doesn't become a gate. On the Natoora SaaS team we agreed one together, and it stayed part of how we worked.”

Write a user story and acceptance criteria

“As a field engineer, I want to submit my weekly timesheet from my phone, so I don't have to drive to the depot. Acceptance criteria: given a completed week, when I submit, it goes to my supervisor for approval and I see its status; given a missing required field, I can't submit and I see a clear message; given I submit twice, only one timesheet is created.”

This example uses the Smart Timesheet context to show the format. The acceptance criteria are illustrations, not a record of the actual backlog.

Scrum or Kanban?

“Scrum when the work benefits from a fixed rhythm and regular stakeholder reviews, for example building a new product. Kanban when work arrives continuously and unpredictably, for example support or BAU change. Often a team uses Scrum for product work and limits work in progress the Kanban way.”

What do you know about SAFe?

“In SAFe, the Product Manager owns the programme backlog and features, and Product Owners own team backlogs and stories. Teams plan together every 8–12 weeks in PI Planning, and priorities use WSJF: cost of delay divided by job size. I haven't worked on a formal Agile Release Train, but at Northern Powergrid I coordinated distributed teams across Chennai, Kolkata and Newcastle around shared dependencies, which is the problem SAFe solves. I'd be happy to take the POPM certification.”

Delivery, technology and risk

What is API-first, and what's your experience?

This is a reported Accenture Digital Product Owner question (see G1 below). Evidence: S3 Smart Timesheet.

“API-first means designing the interface between systems as a contract before building the screens. For a PO, it means agreeing early what data is exchanged, who uses it and what happens when it fails. On Smart Timesheet our OutSystems front end on AWS depended on a .NET integration with Oracle HCM and CRM for employee data, so integration failure, data quality and permissions were backlog items from the start, not surprises at the end.”

What cloud experience do you have?

“Smart Timesheet ran on AWS. At Natoora the SaaS product ran on Google Cloud with Terraform templates, which we reused from Client 1 for Client 2 to cut onboarding time. I passed AWS Certified AI Practitioner in April 2026 and I'm studying for Solutions Architect Associate. As a PO, I care about cloud cost, security and the support model as much as the features.”

How do you balance technical debt against new features?

“I make the cost of the debt visible in business terms — slower delivery, incidents, security risk — and give it a regular share of capacity rather than one big clean-up. On the SaaS product, removing more than 200 hard-coded references and adding tenant isolation was essential debt work: without it, we couldn't safely onboard a second client.”

How do you handle security, compliance and regulation?

“Mandatory obligations go to the top of the backlog with a deadline, and the controls go into the Definition of Done. At Nationwide I delivered regulated banking work such as Open Banking and GDPR, where the date was fixed by regulation. For a public-sector client I'd involve security and data-protection colleagues in refinement, not at the end.”

What risks do you watch in an AI product?

“Wrong outputs, sensitive data leaking, prompt injection — for example an email with hidden instructions — cost, and model changes from the vendor. I manage them with validation, a human on consequential decisions, monitoring of exception rates, and clear ownership of exceptions. On the Natoora tool, validation checked customers and products, and anything uncertain went to a person.”

How do you run a team split across locations?

“At Natoora the SaaS team had nine people across the UK and Brazil. We protected overlap hours for collaboration, wrote decisions down, and ran a joint stand-up plus written end-of-day updates. At Northern Powergrid I ran teams across India and Newcastle. The rule is the same: make work and decisions visible so nobody waits for a meeting.”

Consulting situations

The client changes the objective from profit to quality halfway through. What do you do?

This is a reported Accenture consulting case theme (see G2 below). Answer with the scenario method.

“I'd clarify what ‘quality’ means to them and why it changed now. Then I'd re-order the backlog against the new goal and show which planned work no longer adds value. I'd agree new success measures and check whether the change affects the contract, because a new objective can be a change request. Finally I'd tell the team why priorities moved.”

Stakeholders don't engage or take responsibility. What do you do?

“I find out why — usually unclear benefit, no time or fear of the decision. I make it easy: short decision notes with a recommendation and a deadline, and a demo of working software rather than documents. If a decision still doesn't come, I escalate through the agreed route, presenting it as a risk to their outcome rather than a complaint.”

What would you do in your first 90 days?

“Days 1 to 30: learn the client, users, decision rights and baseline measures — and change very little. Days 31 to 60: tighten the backlog around the product goal and ship one small, valuable release. Days 61 to 90: measure the outcome, adjust the roadmap and set up the mentoring rhythm with the POs and BAs I support.”

Practice questions and answer reasoning

For each drill, give a recommendation, explain what could change it, and name the evidence you would inspect. The outlines below are authored exercises inferred from the role, not employer answers.

Q1: Walk through a deployment you personally owned

Answer outline: Choose one real deployment. Establish the customer workflow, your remit and the baseline. Trace one request across identity, model, data, tool and system-of-record boundaries. Identify the code or configuration you personally changed, the alternative rejected, a failure encountered, and who accepted the service. End with the observed result and measurement limits. Where your role was product or delivery leadership, say that explicitly and use a separately labelled practical exercise to demonstrate current coding capability.

Probe: Which test caught a defect? What did the production trace show? Who could authorise rollback? What did you learn after handoff?

Weak answer: A team achievement told entirely as “I”, or a polished demo without operating evidence.

Q2: Three workstreams need the same integration engineer

Answer outline: Make remaining capacity, dependencies, operational risk and deadlines visible. Protect incident response and essential control work first. Compare the smallest independently valuable slices rather than dividing the specialist's week equally. Recommend a sequence, identify what stops, and take the commercial or scope decision to the authorised sponsor. Pair another engineer on the bottleneck so the capacity problem becomes less dependent on one person. Reforecast using observed throughput.

Probe: What changes if a deadline is contractual? How do you account for support and code review? What evidence justifies moving the engineer again?

Weak answer: Full utilisation as the objective, hidden overtime, or three simultaneous commitments with no contingency.

Q3: An engineer repeatedly misses an agreed quality bar

Answer outline: Discuss specific observable examples privately. Distinguish unclear expectations, missing support, overload and skill gaps before drawing conclusions. Agree the required behaviour, support, review points and a manageable development task. Reduce immediate production risk through pairing or review without public blame. Keep factual records and follow the organisation's people process if improvement does not occur. Discuss longer-term aspirations separately from urgent corrective feedback.

Probe: How would you measure progress? What if this person is the strongest coder but undermines colleagues? When do you involve the people partner?

Weak answer: Claiming delivery coordination proves line management, diagnosing motives, or promising an immediate dismissal.

Q4: The CFO challenges a claim of substantial savings

Answer outline: Separate potential hours released, usable capacity, avoided expenditure, cash savings and revenue. Establish volume, adoption, exception effort, quality and the measurement period. Include implementation, inference, infrastructure, review and support costs. Show base/downside scenarios, the accountable benefit owner and the mechanism by which the benefit can be realised. Recommend continued learning or a hold if the commercial case is not yet evidenced.

Probe: Who validates the calculation? Are review minutes counted twice? What if headcount stays unchanged? Work through the case below.

Weak answer: Multiplying all employees by an optimistic time saving and describing it as profit.

Q5: The CTO wants speed while the CISO restricts data movement

Answer outline: Clarify the exact data classification, permitted processing locations and actions. Bring the workflow and service owners into one decision. Compare a narrow approved-data slice, private managed access and self-hosted inference against operational cost and required controls. Recommend one reversible experiment with acceptance criteria. Present the executive decision first, then use an architecture walkthrough or code-with session to demonstrate the riskiest assumption with engineers.

Probe: Who accepts residual risk? Which records stay out of the model? What remains blocked even if the demo succeeds?

Weak answer: Treating private connectivity as proof of complete compliance or promising that a prompt prevents disclosure.

Q6: Your provider fails halfway through an action workflow

Answer outline: Identify whether generation, a tool proposal or an external write failed. A replacement provider must pass the same task-quality and data-policy gates and support the required contract. If a write's outcome is unknown, reconcile using operation identity before any replay. Do not send protected data to an unapproved provider to preserve availability. Bound retries and cost, persist state, and surface a recoverable hold when no safe route exists.

Probe: How do tool-call IDs, streaming events, structured output and usage accounting differ? What happens if the second provider lacks a required feature?

Weak answer: “They all use an OpenAI-compatible API, so just switch the URL.”

Q7: Your retrieval assistant is fluent but wrong

Answer outline: Collect representative failed queries and segment by consequence. Check whether the authoritative source exists, is fresh and is permitted for this user. Measure retrieval independently of answer generation; inspect ranking and context assembly before changing prompts. Test abstention and escalation. Use held-out cases and regression checks, then compare downstream completion and correction effort.

Probe: What if top-k recall improves but operators take longer? How would you test cross-tenant leakage? How do you avoid tuning against the final test set?

Weak answer: Adding more documents or a larger context window without identifying the failing stage.

Q8: Choose serverless, a container service or microservices

Answer outline: Start with runtime duration, burstiness, concurrency, private connectivity, state, operational skills and recovery requirements. A short stateless handler may fit serverless; long-running or specialised processes may fit managed containers. Keep durable waiting outside a request process. Split services only when an ownership, scaling or isolation boundary justifies the extra deployment and failure paths.
