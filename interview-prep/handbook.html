Prepare in three passes

  1. Match: identify the five strongest requirements in the advert; match each to a story and a decision you actually made.
  2. Explain: rehearse a 90-second answer; prepare the artefact, measurement and alternative for follow-up questions.
  3. Challenge: practise a recovery or prioritisation scenario, a setback and a credibility probe. Ask what the panel could challenge in your wording.

If only two hours remain, spend 20 minutes decoding the advert, 50 minutes on three stories, 25 minutes on a scenario, 15 minutes on motivation and questions, and 10 minutes checking logistics.

Shared interview handbook

Status: Draft for owner review · Last reviewed: 1 October 2026

Use this handbook to explain your judgement. Choose relevant methods and artefacts, connect them to what you did, and show how the outcome changed. Definitions live here; role guides apply them to different responsibilities. Historical facts live in the evidence bank.

Decode the advert before rehearsing

For each important requirement, write: intended outcome; expected decision authority; user or stakeholder; matching example; gap or adjacent experience; likely follow-up. Identify whether the employer needs a product decision-maker, a delivery leader or a project controller. Titles vary, so test the remit with the recruiter.

Distinguish three levels honestly: direct experience, adjacent experience, and knowledge you would apply with support. Certifications demonstrate learning; formal role accountabilities still need examples.

Behavioural answers

Use Situation → Responsibility → Decision and Action → Result → Learning. A familiar STAR structure is also suitable if the decision and learning are explicit.

  1. Give enough context to explain the difficulty.
  2. State your remit and authority.
  3. Describe what you personally decided, the alternative, the trade-off and the action.
  4. Explain the result, its measurement and any limit to attribution.
  5. State what you changed afterwards or would do differently.

Prepare a 90-second version and a deeper follow-up. An artefact matters because it enabled a decision: a RACI clarified approvals, a RAID log surfaced a blocking dependency, or a prototype overturned an assumption. Naming a method alone does not establish competence.

Use several stories across an interview. Recent Natoora examples show current product and delivery capability; Northern Powergrid adds field adoption and infrastructure complexity; banking examples add regulated and supplier experience. Choose relevance over recency when a question demands a specific kind of evidence.

Scenario answers

For a hypothetical, explain assumptions → immediate protection → diagnosis → options → decision → sequence → measures. Ask a few questions about users, impact, constraints and authority, then proceed with stated assumptions.

If a team is six weeks late after losing its lead, address immediate production, contractual or people risks straight away. Diagnose in parallel through team and stakeholder conversations, current commitments, delivery evidence and dependencies. Establish a small stabilisation plan with owners and dates; then agree a realistic forecast and improvement experiments. A first week of listening must not delay urgent protection.

Define progress in outcomes: reduced blocked time, clearer decisions, a reliable forecast, lower incident exposure or restored client confidence. Avoid promising that a particular ceremony, framework or headcount increase will repair every situation.

Use these as choices, not a mandatory stack. Story points do not measure customer value. Definition of Ready is an optional team practice, not a required Scrum artefact. Change governance should match the impact and client context; CAB approval is not universal.

Applying the toolkit

Decision rights and stakeholders. RACI means Responsible, Accountable, Consulted and Informed. Map a small set of consequential decisions, not every activity. Confirm one accountable decision owner and the route when a decision exceeds their authority. A power/interest map can guide communication, but include affected users with little organisational power. In an answer, explain which delayed or duplicated decision the artefact resolved.

Prioritisation. MoSCoW distinguishes Must, Should, Could and Won't for the agreed time horizon. A Must requires a clear consequence if omitted; “the stakeholder wants it” is insufficient. WSJF compares cost of delay with job duration or a size proxy. In the SAFe formulation, cost of delay combines relative user/business value, time criticality and risk reduction/opportunity enablement. Dependencies and mandatory controls still constrain the order. Kano can help discuss basic needs, performance expectations and possible delights; validate those categories with users.

Illustrative WSJF exercise: option A has relative cost of delay 12 and size 3, giving 4; option B has cost of delay 15 and size 5, giving 3. On that limited evidence A comes first. Explain the scoring uncertainty, whether B enables A and whether either is mandatory. This is a reasoning exercise, not a historical prioritisation decision.

Risk and forecasting. RAID records Risks, Assumptions, Issues and Dependencies. A risk needs a response, owner and trigger; an issue needs action now. A pre-mortem asks why a future failure might happen and converts plausible causes into controls or experiments. Critical-path analysis identifies the dependency chain controlling completion under the current plan. Resource constraints or uncertainty can change it. A burn-up separates completed work from total scope; widening scope can explain why a stable completion rate still misses the date.

Change and coaching. ADKAR considers Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability and Reinforcement. Diagnose the actual barrier: another training session does not fix a lack of time or tools to use the skill. For organisation-wide change, a coalition, clear purpose, removal of practical barriers, visible wins and reinforcement can help; do not present change as a rigid sequence that everyone experiences identically. Adapt coaching to the task and person's support needs, with feedback and a review point.

Facilitation. In 1-2-4-All, start with individual reflection, then pairs, fours and whole-group sharing to widen participation. “What, so what, now what” separates observation, interpretation and next action. Five Whys can produce a causal hypothesis, but verify it against evidence and avoid forcing every incident into one cause. Close with an owner, action and review date. The interview value is the better decision or changed behaviour, not the workshop name.

Commercial contracts. Time and materials, fixed-price and managed-service arrangements create different incentives and obligations. Establish the actual contract before discussing scope, capacity or billing. Prepare one example where a change process, milestone acceptance or forecast enabled a fair decision. Claim margin or utilisation ownership only if it was genuinely part of your remit.

Scrum, Kanban and scaled delivery

In Scrum, the Product Owner is accountable for maximising value and effective Product Backlog management. Delegation does not transfer that accountability. The team collaborates on the Sprint Goal; Developers select and plan the work they can complete. The Scrum Master supports effectiveness and self-management. A review inspects outcomes and adapts direction; it is not merely a status demonstration.

Use Kanban thinking to understand work in progress, throughput, cycle time, ageing work and blocked time. Make workflow policies explicit, then test a change against the actual bottleneck. Local productivity gains can leave end-to-end flow unchanged.

For scaled delivery, explain the underlying problem: objectives, dependencies, capacity, integration, decision rights and shared outcomes. In a SAFe context, learn the employer's actual ART, PI planning and backlog arrangements. Product Management leads strategy and roadmapping across the ART; team-level ownership has a different remit. Do not retrospectively label multi-team work as a formal SAFe implementation.

Public sector and regulated work

Know the distinction between the GDS Service Standard, Technology Code of Practice, user-centred design practice and formal assessment experience. Discovery investigates the problem and whether to proceed; alpha tests risky assumptions through prototypes; beta builds and tests the service; live includes continuing improvement and operations. Retirement is also part of lifecycle ownership.

For a government service, consider whole journeys across channels, accessibility and assisted digital support, privacy, security, performance and operational reliability. The Service Manual identifies cost per transaction, user satisfaction, completion and digital take-up as service KPIs; add measures for the specific service. Confirm which standards and publication requirements apply to the assignment.

In banking or utilities, use a real regulatory or operational constraint rather than claiming government delivery by association. In an AI service, consider data suitability, privacy, evaluation, user recourse, exception handling and monitoring. Define release thresholds with the relevant specialists and accountable owner.

Rehearsal and self-review

Score each answer from 0–2 for relevance, personal ownership, judgement, evidence, calibration and clarity. A zero identifies a concrete rehearsal gap. Ask a partner to probe your metric, the rejected alternative and who held authority. This is a practice rubric, not an Accenture or Civil Service scoring scale.

Before an interview, confirm format, panel, case or presentation, duration and logistics. Prepare a specific motivation, three questions, a setback and a truthful current-status answer. Future interview formats are not established by the stages used in an earlier application.

S1 GenAI order automation

Context: Natoora email-to-order workflow. Customer support previously read each incoming order email and manually entered it into the system, typically taking 2–3 minutes per order.

Owner-confirmed outcome: approximately 90% reduction in manual effort. Exceptions remained when the agent could not complete an order. This is not a claim of 90% model accuracy or 90% of orders completed without intervention.

Owner-confirmed involvement: rollout in London and Paris. Six-region deployment was a wider target; completion in other regions is not established by this record. The portfolio places this work in March–September 2025.

Decisions to discuss: manageable pilot scope; customer-support participation; exception handling; integration with the order system; quality and cost trade-offs; rollout readiness and learning from operational feedback. Explain your decision rights and your collaboration with the developer.

Metric defence: describe how manual effort was measured, baseline workload, period and scope of comparison, exceptions and review effort. Time released is an operational benefit; cash savings require a separate cost calculation.

Technical boundary: an OpenAI model extracted order information with validation and exception handling. The exact model ID remains to be confirmed; use model-neutral wording. Do not claim zero risk, eliminated errors or an independently calibrated confidence score without evidence.

Reference: GenAI case study.

S2 SaaS transformation

Context: Natoora's internal warehouse platform was adapted for external customers. The portfolio describes a nine-person UK/Brazil team and two customer implementations in January 2024–May 2025.

Owner-confirmed commercial figures: £28k setup fee, £1.6k recurring monthly revenue, and £29k reported profit in the first five months following rollout. Recurring revenue supported cloud operations, developer support and other customer expenses. The owner was not in charge of the initiative after that period. Delivery Lead interview guide

Status: Draft for owner review · Last reviewed: 1 October 2026

Show how you create dependable delivery across teams, clients and operations. A senior answer connects delivery health, decisions, commercial constraints and business outcomes. Reporting status is useful only when it changes what happens next.

Positioning and evidence

A useful opening: “I have led product and technology delivery across utilities, banking and operational software. My approach is to make risks and dependencies visible, establish clear decisions and sequence delivery around business outcomes. I combine technical understanding with stakeholder coordination and operational readiness.” Follow with one real recovery or scope decision, rather than a list of technologies.

Questions to rehearse

  1. How do you know delivery is healthy? Combine outcome progress, work flow, quality, dependency age, decision latency, team capacity and release readiness. Explain which threshold triggers an action. Avoid a dashboard with no accountable owner.
  2. Tell me about a programme you recovered. State the original commitment, evidence of risk, what you protected immediately and the diagnosis. Compare scope/date/capacity choices, obtain the relevant decision and show a measurable recovery. Use an actual case; do not retrofit the hypothetical below into history.
  3. What if the client demands an unrealistic date? Clarify the business event and contractual obligation. Test a minimum viable scope, sequencing, dependencies and quality constraints. Present options and consequences; record the agreed decision and confidence range.
  4. How do you manage multiple suppliers? Establish deliverables, interface owners, acceptance evidence, escalation paths and shared milestones. Surface dependencies before they become blame. Commercial changes require the appropriate authority.
  5. How do you manage delivery economics? Distinguish budget, forecast, burn, margin and benefits. Examine scope growth, rework and capacity assumptions. Explain what you controlled and what finance or the account lead approved.
  6. How do you lead distributed teams? Describe protected overlap, asynchronous decisions, clear ownership, equitable participation and handling missed hand-offs. Give a real example where the working arrangement changed an outcome.
  7. How do you ensure rollout and support readiness? Include support ownership, training, observability, runbooks, rollback, acceptance and operational capacity. Treat go-live as a transition into a supported service.
  8. How do you handle poor performance or conflict? Diagnose expectations and constraints, give specific feedback, agree support and follow-up, and escalate fairly if needed. Do not substitute velocity comparisons for a performance conversation.

Worked commercial answer

The SaaS case connects a nine-person UK/Brazil team with client onboarding and launch constraints. A defensible answer explains a particular decision: how you narrowed launch scope, managed competing capacity or applied learning to a subsequent implementation. The portfolio reports implementation cost falling from £9k to £7k and elapsed delivery from four months to two. Present these as separate comparisons and acknowledge scope differences.

The £28k setup fee and £1.6k monthly recurring revenue were revenue; recurring revenue supported ongoing customer costs. The £29k reported profit covers the first five months after rollout, and your responsibility ended after that period. Prepare the relevant customer/period and cost definition rather than inventing a margin calculation.

Recovery scenario — illustrative, not historical

You inherit delivery six weeks before a contractual milestone. Two dependencies are late, test defects are rising, the plan is green and the team doubts it. Protect urgent safety, security and customer risks immediately. In parallel, validate completed work, remaining effort, dependency dates, capacity and acceptance criteria with the teams. Identify the true critical path and compare a reduced milestone, changed sequence/date and targeted additional capacity. Explain the consequences and who decides.

Publish a credible forecast and recovery actions with owners, decision dates and evidence of completion. Use short review cycles focused on critical work and quality; update the client without concealing uncertainty. Monitor defect trend, dependency closure and accepted scope. Learn why reporting and reality diverged. “Spend the first week observing without acting” is inadequate when urgent risks exist.

Technical and governance probes

Be ready to explain why a migration requires dependency discovery, rollback and business validation; why AI needs exception handling and evaluation; and why multi-tenancy affects access, isolation and support. Your technical depth should illuminate a delivery decision. State when the architect, engineer, security lead or sponsor made the design/approval decision.

Expect: Which commitment did you change? What did the client disagree with? Who signed off risk? What did you escalate and when? What was the financial effect? How was benefit realised after launch?

First 90 days and panel questions

Initially establish commitments, clients, decision rights, delivery health and urgent risks. Next improve one significant constraint and stabilise a credible forecast. Then strengthen cross-team planning, operational transition and benefits ownership based on measured results. Adapt the sequence if delivery is already in crisis.

Ask: What commercial commitments shape this role? Which decisions can the lead make? Where do dependencies commonly fail? How do account, product and delivery responsibilities interact? Who owns benefits after handover?

Rehearsal: one recovery, one scope negotiation, one supplier conflict, one commercial decision, one leadership setback and one operational transition. Use the shared handbook for frameworks and answer scoring.

Agile Delivery Lead interview guide

Status: Draft for owner review · Last reviewed: 1 October 2026

Show how you help teams deliver useful outcomes through better flow, collaboration and learning. Seniority appears in coaching judgement, cross-team coordination and removing structural constraints. Running ceremonies alone does not demonstrate that impact.

Positioning and evidence

A useful opening: “My experience combines Agile delivery leadership with product ownership and technical delivery. I help teams make work and dependencies visible, improve collaboration and inspect whether changes actually improve delivery. I adapt the working approach to the service and constraints rather than treating a framework as the objective.” Add a concrete improvement with a baseline and result you can defend.

EvidenceWhat to demonstrate
S4 infrastructureRisk-based sequencing, operations dependencies and mixed work types
S2 SaaSUK/Brazil collaboration, capacity transparency and reusable learning
S3 Smart TimesheetWorking with product priorities and users; actual role was PO
S1 order automationPilot learning, operational feedback and adoption

Questions to rehearse

  1. When would you choose Scrum or Kanban? Consider the nature of work, interruption rate, planning horizon and team needs. Scrum provides goals and bounded inspection cycles; Kanban makes flow explicit and controls work in progress. Explain the relevant practices and review evidence rather than declaring one universally superior.
  2. How do you improve predictability? Make work and policies visible, reduce excessive work in progress, split work, expose blockers and use observed completion data. Report uncertainty and dependencies. Predictability must preserve quality and user value.
  3. What metrics do you use? Explain lead/cycle time boundaries, throughput, work-item age, WIP and quality measures. Discuss trends and variability. Velocity is team-specific planning information, not a cross-team ranking or productivity target.
  4. How do you facilitate a difficult retrospective? Create safety, use concrete evidence, invite quieter voices, distinguish systemic constraints from blame and choose a small experiment with an owner and review date. Explain what changed in a real example.
  5. How do you coach a team resisting change? Understand the problem and their experience, co-design a low-risk experiment, make the benefit visible and inspect it. Explain when a policy is mandatory and when the team has choice.
  6. How do you handle repeated sprint spillover? Examine work slicing, scope changes, hidden work, dependencies, quality and capacity. Collaborate with the PO and Developers on a realistic Sprint Goal and planning approach. Do not simply demand higher estimates or overtime.
  7. How do you coordinate several teams? Map shared outcomes, dependencies, interface owners and decision dates. Resolve the constraint closest to the work; use programme forums for decisions beyond team authority. Attendance at a scaled ceremony alone is not evidence of leading scaled delivery.
  8. How do you work with a PO, engineering lead and manager? Clarify accountability for value, technical quality, people support and delivery facilitation. Surface trade-offs jointly. Avoid becoming the sole approver or a messenger for every decision.

Coaching answer exercise

Select a real case of excessive work in progress, delayed decisions or poor hand-offs. State your observation, the team's interpretation, a practice you agreed and the result. The SaaS portfolio describes distributed working and reusable onboarding; explain which parts you personally established and how you assessed them. The two-client cost and duration comparison is useful evidence, but does not prove that ceremonies caused a productivity increase.

Use: “We observed [specific problem]. I facilitated [actual discussion/experiment]. We changed [policy/working arrangement]. We checked [measure] over [period], and learned [result/next change].” Complete this with real evidence before rehearsal.

Flow scenario — illustrative, not historical

Ten items are in progress, only two finish each week, and urgent support work repeatedly interrupts feature delivery. Clarify whether support and planned work share skills and capacity. Visualise all work and item age; establish explicit urgency criteria. Test lower WIP, smaller slices, clearer intake or a reserved support approach with the team. Avoid making a new support lane a dumping ground that conceals work.

Use throughput and observed cycle-time distribution to discuss the likely completion range. Review quality, user outcomes and team experience as guardrails. If the bottleneck is another department's approval, coach locally while escalating that structural constraint with evidence. Do not promise a fixed percentage improvement before observing the system.

Seniority and credibility probes

Prepare examples of influencing outside your team, coaching another practitioner, challenging an unhelpful metric and changing your own approach after feedback. State actual mentoring scope: formal mentoring, peer coaching and team facilitation are related but different experiences.

Expect: How did the team choose the change? What resistance did you encounter? What would have happened without it? How did you distinguish correlation from causation? Did improvement endure after you stepped back? When did you escalate rather than facilitate?

If asked about SAFe, explain the environment you actually used, team and programme backlog responsibilities and dependency planning. Do not invent PI Planning experience. Use the shared handbook for current role distinctions.

First 90 days and panel questions

Learn the service, team agreements, demand, dependencies and baseline flow first. Agree one focused improvement experiment next. Then assess the result, build team capability and tackle cross-team constraints with leadership. Preserve useful existing practices; change what the evidence justifies.

Ask: What slows teams most today? How much influence does this role have beyond the team? Which metrics trigger decisions? How are operational interruptions handled? What coaching support exists for other delivery practitioners?

Rehearsal: one facilitation conflict, one flow improvement, one coaching example, one cross-team dependency and one failed experiment. Each needs a personal action and evidence, not a ceremony list.
