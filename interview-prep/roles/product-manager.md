# Product Manager interview guide

**Status:** Draft for owner review · **Last reviewed:** 1 October 2026

Prepare to defend which problems deserve investment, for which users, and how the product can produce sustainable outcomes. The emphasis is broader discovery, strategy and economics. Actual decision rights vary by organisation; establish them before assuming the PM controls funding or the PO only writes stories.

## Positioning and evidence

A useful opening: “My product and delivery background has involved improving operational services, adapting an internal platform for external customers and rolling out AI-enabled order processing. I connect user problems with delivery feasibility and commercial consequences. I would bring that experience to product discovery, prioritisation and learning after launch.” State your actual historical titles and the product decisions you personally owned.

## Questions to rehearse

1. **How would you build a product strategy?** Identify the segment, important unmet need, competing alternatives, organisation's strengths and constraints. State a few strategic choices, an outcome and the assumptions that could invalidate them. Explain what you will stop doing.
2. **How do you discover a valuable problem?** Combine user interviews and observation with service data and commercial evidence. Avoid equating a stakeholder feature request with validated demand. Separate desirability, feasibility, viability and operational constraints.
3. **How do you construct a roadmap?** Organise around outcomes and problems, make dependencies visible and distinguish near-term commitments from uncertain future options. Set evidence-based review points. A roadmap is not a promise that every item will ship on a fixed date.
4. **How would you measure product-market fit?** Specify the market and business model. Look for repeated use, retention, willingness to pay and customer outcomes. Enterprise sales cycles and implementation services change the evidence you can obtain. One successful client is useful learning, not universal proof.
5. **When would you stop a product or feature?** Define a hypothesis and threshold before investment. Include strategic fit, opportunity cost, customer commitments, migration and support obligations. Describe a real stop/defer decision if available; otherwise label the example hypothetical.
6. **How do you work with engineering and design?** Co-discover the problem and risks, involve engineers early, test alternatives cheaply, and preserve their responsibility for technical design. Explain how learning affects the scope rather than handing over a fixed solution.
7. **How do you make an AI product decision?** Compare rules, conventional software and model-based approaches against representative user tasks. Consider error consequences, validation, exception handling, data rights, latency, cost and operational ownership. Use outcome measures alongside model evaluation.
8. **How do you balance growth and reliability?** Show the cost of failure, support load, retained customers and the marginal value of another feature. Agree guardrails and choose a sustainable investment mix.

## Commercial worked example

For SaaS, explain the opportunity to adapt a proven internal warehouse platform for external customers. The owner-confirmed figures are £28k setup, £1.6k recurring monthly revenue and £29k reported profit in the first five months after rollout. The recurring revenue supported cloud operations, developer support and other expenses. Your responsibility ended after that period; do not extrapolate later profitability.

Discuss one choice about target customer, onboarding scope or reusable capability. State who approved pricing and investment. Prepare the cost basis and customer coverage for a financial follow-up; the guide does not reconstruct missing accounting records. Compare £9k and £7k implementation cost separately from four and two months elapsed delivery time. Scope differences matter to both comparisons.

## Product case — illustrative, not historical

“Should the warehouse platform expand to another customer segment?” Start with the decision and ask about segment size, current alternatives, common workflows, buying process and constraints. Interview a small representative group; analyse recurring needs and customisation burden. Test a proposition through demos or a bounded pilot before building a broad feature set. Assess recurring revenue, cloud/support costs, onboarding investment and risk to the core business. Choose proceed, narrow, defer or stop with a clear evidence threshold and accountable sponsor.

If asked for a metric tree, connect the outcome to drivers: successful customer operations depend on activated customers, reliable daily use and task completion. Revenue depends on pricing and retained paying customers; contribution depends on revenue less the relevant costs. Agree definitions before adding targets. No arbitrary percentage target is evidence of strategy.

## Credibility probes

What was your hypothesis before delivery? Which users did you speak to? How did an insight change the roadmap? What alternative would have solved the problem? What did you decide personally? Were financial results realised or forecast? Which failure or metric would change your recommendation?

For AI, remember that the confirmed result is a reduction in **manual effort** during London/Paris rollout. It is not an accuracy measure or proof that a fixed share of orders needed no human work.

## First 90 days and panel questions

First understand the market, users, strategy, economics and decision rights. Next validate the riskiest assumption and align a small set of outcome measures. Then recommend roadmap changes using evidence from customers and operations, with engineering/design feasibility and stakeholder agreement.

Ask: Which product choices can this role make? How are discovery and delivery funded? What evidence informs investment reviews? How is support cost represented in product decisions? Which assumption in the current strategy worries the team most?

**Rehearsal:** one discovery story, one strategy trade-off, one commercial case, one failed assumption and one product case. Use the shared handbook for methods and Cracking the PM Career for supplementary practice; keep this guide focused on your evidence and the vacancy.
