Project management

Delivering a defined outcome within constraints. PMBOK 8, Agile, PRINCE2 7 and the core control maths.

First principles

The problem it solves: an organisation needs a defined change delivered to an agreed scope, date and budget, and the people paying for it need to see and steer progress.

TruthSo what
Scope, time, cost and quality are linked; one cannot change alone.Every change request gets an impact assessment and a decision, not a quiet yes.
Uncertainty is highest at the start and falls as work is done.Estimate in ranges, hold contingency against named risks, and re-forecast as evidence arrives.
People decide faster with clear authority and early bad news.Agree tolerances and escalation routes up front; report by exception, with options.
A project ends; the benefit it was funded for usually arrives later.Name a business owner for each benefit and plan the handover from the start.

Constraints

Triple constraint

KeyScope, time and cost trade off, with quality in the middle. Predictive fixes scope; Agile fixes time and cost and lets scope flex.

Extended view: scope, schedule, cost, quality, resources, risk. A change to one forces a change to at least one other, which is why every change request needs an impact analysis.

KeyVisualise work, limit WIP, manage flow. Cycle time = WIP ÷ throughput, so cutting WIP is the fastest way to speed up delivery.

Practices

Flow metrics

  • Cycle time: work started to done (team view).
  • Throughput: items finished per period.
  • CFD: widening bands mean a bottleneck; flat lines mean blocked work.

Schedule · Critical Path Method

Critical path, live

KeyThe critical path is the longest path through the network, so it sets the shortest possible duration. Activities on it have zero total float.

Change any duration (days) to watch the forward pass, backward pass and critical path recalculate.

  • Forward pass: ES = max(EF of predecessors); EF = ES + duration.
  • Backward pass: LF = min(LS of successors); LS = LF − duration.
  • Total float = LS − ES. Free float = ES of next − EF.
  • Crashing: add resources to critical tasks. Costs money.
  • Fast-tracking: overlap tasks in parallel. Adds risk and rework.
  • Dependencies: FS (most common), SS, FF, SF. Lead = overlap, lag = wait.
Cost · Earned Value Management

Earned value calculator

KeyEV is the budgeted value of work actually done. Compare it with AC for cost (CPI) and with PV for schedule (SPI). Below 1 is bad, above 1 is good.

Against BAC

Memory aid: EV comes first in every variance and index. Variance = EV minus something; index = EV divided by something.

Estimating

Three-point (PERT)

KeyPERT weights the most likely value 4×: (O + 4M + P) ÷ 6. Standard deviation is (P − O) ÷ 6.

Other techniques

  • Analogous: top-down from a similar past project. Fast, least accurate.
  • Parametric: unit rate × quantity (e.g. £/m², hours/screen).
  • Bottom-up: sum the WBS work packages. Slow, most accurate.
  • Relative (Agile): story points via Planning Poker (Fibonacci), T-shirt sizes, affinity grouping.
  • Velocity forecast: remaining points ÷ average velocity = Sprints left.

Rough order of magnitude: −25% to +75%. Definitive: −5% to +10%.

Uncertainty · Risk

Risk matrix & responses

KeyRisk = uncertain event with a positive (opportunity) or negative (threat) effect. Score probability × impact, then pick a response. An issue is a risk that has happened.

Tap a cell · probability (rows) × impact (columns)
ThreatOpportunity
Avoid: change the plan to remove itExploit: make sure it happens
Transfer: insurance, fixed-price contractShare: partner with a better-placed party
Mitigate: reduce probability or impactEnhance: increase probability or impact
Accept (active = contingency reserve; passive = do nothing) · Escalate when outside the project's authority
Expected monetary value · EMV = P × impact

Contingency reserve covers known-unknowns (identified risks, PM controls it, in the cost baseline). Management reserve covers unknown-unknowns (sponsor controls it, outside the baseline). Secondary risks arise from a response; residual risks remain after it.

Quality · change control

Quality & change

KeyPrevention beats inspection. Every change request goes through impact analysis and a decision before baselines change.

Integrated change control

2AnalyseImpact on scope, time, cost, risk
3DecideCCB or sponsor
4UpdateBaselines, plans, log
5TellCommunicate

Cost of quality

Conformance
  • Prevention: training, standards
  • Appraisal: testing, inspection
Non-conformance
  • Internal failure: rework, scrap
  • External failure: returns, liability, reputation

Tools

  • Pareto: 80% of defects from 20% of causes.
  • Fishbone (Ishikawa) and 5 Whys for root cause.
  • Control chart: rule of seven (7 points on one side of the mean = investigate).
  • PDCA: Plan, Do, Check, Act.

Quality = fit for purpose and meets requirements. Grade = category of features. Low grade can be fine; low quality never is.

Business case · benefits

Project selection

KeyPick the higher NPV, IRR, ROI and BCR, and the shorter payback. NPV wins when they disagree; project length is already priced in.

MeasureRule
NPV = Σ CFt/(1+r)t − investment> 0 accept; higher better
IRR (rate where NPV = 0)> cost of capital
ROI = (gain − cost) ÷ costHigher better
BCR = benefits ÷ costs> 1
Payback periodShorter better; ignores later cash

Opportunity cost = value of the best option not chosen. Sunk costs are never a reason to continue. Benefits realisation often lands after the project closes, so name an owner.

Feynman loop

  1. Blank page: redraw the control loop from memory: business case, baselines, deliver, measure, tolerance gate, exception, close. Number the arrows.
  2. Explain it to a junior: a supplier says a key component will be four weeks late. Explain what you do next, and why.
    Banned words: critical path, baseline, escalate
  3. Gap hunt: unsure whether the delay moves the end date? Reread Critical path, live. Unsure of the cost effect? Reread Earned value calculator. Unsure who decides? Reread tolerances in PRINCE2: 7 × 3 and Quality & change.
Model answer (open after you have tried)

First I check whether that component sits on the chain of tasks that sets the finish date. If it has slack, the end date may not move: I record it and watch it. If it has none, the whole project slips unless we act. With the supplier and the team I work out options: run some work in parallel, add people, cut or postpone part of the scope, or accept the delay. Each has a cost and a risk. If the slip is bigger than the limits the sponsor agreed I could absorb, I take it to them straight away with the options and my recommendation, rather than waiting for the next report. Whatever is decided goes into the plan, so everyone works to the same dates.

Product management

Finding the right problem, the right solution and evidence that it works. Discovery, prioritisation, metrics, economics and evaluation.

First principles

The problem it solves: a team can build almost anything; the hard part is choosing what is worth building and proving that it made a difference.

TruthSo what
Customers pay for progress on their problem, not for features.Start from an outcome and the customer's job, and judge every idea against them.
Many ideas do not deliver the value their authors expect.Test the riskiest assumption cheaply before committing a team.
Capacity is always smaller than the backlog.Prioritise explicitly and say what you will not do.
A product matters only if it moves a measurable outcome, at a sustainable cost.Set a target and guardrails before release, measure after it, and check the unit economics.

Discovery · Design Council

Double Diamond

KeyDiverge then converge twice: first on the problem, then on the solution. Most failed products skip the first diamond.

  • Discover: interviews, observation, data. Don't solve yet.
  • Define: one sharp problem statement; "How might we…?"
  • Develop: many options, prototypes, co-design.
  • Deliver: test, refine, launch, measure.
Discovery · Marty Cagan

Four product risks

KeyBefore building, test value, usability, feasibility and viability. The product trio (PM, designer, tech lead) splits ownership.

ValueWill customers buy or choose it? · PM
ViabilityDoes it work for the business: legal, finance, brand? · PM
UsabilityCan users figure it out? · Designer
FeasibilityCan we build it with our time, skills and tech? · Engineering

Assumption mapping: plot assumptions by importance × evidence. Test the important, low-evidence ones first.

Continuous discovery · Teresa Torres

Opportunity Solution Tree

KeyStart from one measurable outcome, map customer opportunities (needs, pains, desires), then compare several solutions per opportunity and test their assumptions.

Rhythm: weekly customer touchpoints by the product trio. Opportunities come from interviews, not from feature requests.

Customer insight

Jobs to be Done

KeyPeople "hire" a product to make progress in a situation. Design for the job and its context, not for a demographic persona.

When I'm planning dinner on a busy weeknight,
I want to reorder what worked last time,

Forces of progress

Help the switch
  • Push of the current situation
  • Pull of the new solution
Block the switch

  • Habit of the present

Jobs have functional, emotional and social dimensions. Switch interviews ask about the last time someone changed solution.

Kano model

KeyBasics only prevent frustration; performance features scale satisfaction linearly; delighters surprise. Today's delighter is tomorrow's basic.

Leading metrics (activation) predict lagging ones (revenue). Vanity metrics (total sign-ups, page views) only go up; actionable metrics change decisions. NPS = % promoters (9–10) − % detractors (0–6).

Product–market fit

Do we have PMF?

KeyTwo signals: 40%+ of users would be "very disappointed" without the product (Sean Ellis), and the retention curve flattens instead of decaying to zero.

Ask the survey only of users who have experienced the core value (e.g. used it twice in the last two weeks).

Economics

Unit economics

KeyLTV = ARPU × gross margin ÷ churn. Healthy SaaS benchmark: LTV:CAC ≥ 3 and CAC payback under 12 months.

Average customer lifetime ≈ 1 ÷ churn. Levers: raise ARPU (pricing, upsell), cut churn (onboarding, value), cut CAC (referral, organic). Net revenue retention above 100% means expansion outpaces churn.

Experimentation

A/B tests & hypotheses

KeyWrite the hypothesis and success threshold before the test. Small effects need large samples, roughly 16 · p(1−p) ÷ δ² users per variant.

We believe one-tap reorder
will raise repeat orders
for returning customers.
We'll know when repeat rate rises 5pp
in 4 weeks.
  • Significance 95% (p < 0.05): false positive risk 5%.
  • Power 80%: chance of detecting a real effect.
  • Guardrail metrics: things that must not get worse (e.g. refunds).
  • Don't stop early when it "looks significant".

Feynman loop

  1. Blank page: redraw outcome, opportunities, solutions, assumption test, release and measure from memory, with both learning loops.
  2. Explain it to a junior: a senior stakeholder asks you to add their favourite feature to the next release. Explain how you decide.
    Banned words: RICE, MVP, outcome
  3. Gap hunt: unsure how to compare it with planned work? Reread Prioritisation lab. Unsure how to test it cheaply? Reread Four product risks and Build–Measure–Learn. Unsure how you would know it worked? Reread Metrics that matter.
Model answer (open after you have tried)

I start by asking what problem the feature solves, for whom, and what we would see change if it worked. Then I compare it roughly with what is already planned: how many people it helps, how much, how sure we are, and how much work it takes. If the case is uncertain, I look for a quick, cheap way to check the biggest guess, such as showing a mock-up to five customers or adding a button that measures interest before anything is built. If it earns its place, it goes in and something else moves out, and I explain that trade-off openly. If it does not, I explain why using the same comparison, and agree what evidence would change the answer.

Evaluation · before build

Opportunity scorecard

KeyScore an idea against consistent criteria before committing a team. The weakest criterion tells you which assumption to test next.

Rate 1 (weak) to 5 (strong) · example idea: one-tap reorder

Cagan's four questions (Inspired, 2nd ed.)

  1. Business objective: which objective does this serve?
  2. Key results: how will we know it succeeded?
  3. Customer problem: what problem does it solve for customers?
  4. Target market: which customers are we focused on?

The criteria here are a generic starting set. Adapt them, and weights if you use any, to your strategy.

Evaluation · after launch

Post-launch verdict

KeyCompare the result with the target set before launch, check guardrails, then decide: scale, iterate or roll back.

Example: repeat-order rate as the primary metric, refund rate as the guardrail.

Timing and traps

  • Adoption in weeks 1–2; outcome at 4–8 weeks; retention at 3 months.
  • Novelty effect: early spikes often fade, so re-measure.
  • Seasonality: compare with a holdout group, not just last month.
  • Feature adoption = users of the feature ÷ active users.
  • Pair numbers with qualitative feedback to learn why.

SUS calculator · 1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree

Odd items (positive) score answer − 1. Even items (negative) score 5 − answer. Sum × 2.5 gives 0–100. Roughly: below 51 poor, 68 average, above 80 excellent (top 10%).

Evaluation · AI features

Evaluating AI features (evals)

KeyAI output varies from run to run, so a good demo proves little. Build an eval set of real examples, set pass thresholds before release, and keep evaluating in production.

What to measure

QualityTask success, accuracy against expected answers, groundedness (claims supported by sources), hallucination rate
ClassificationPrecision, recall, F1 (calculator alongside)
UserAcceptance rate, edit rate, thumbs up/down, hand-offs to a human
OperationalLatency, cost per task, safety and policy violations

How to score outputs

  • Code checks: deterministic tests such as valid format, required fields, citations present.
  • LLM-as-judge: a model scores outputs against a rubric. Check it agrees with human labels before you trust it.
  • Human review: experts grade samples or compare two versions side by side.

Re-run the full eval set whenever the model, prompt, data or retrieval changes. That makes it a regression test.

Feynman · explain it back

Explain to a sponsor why a feature that hit its launch target might still be rolled back.
Banned words: guardrail, A/B test, metric

Model answer (open after you have tried)

Before launch we agree two kinds of number: the one we are trying to improve, and the ones that must not get worse, such as refunds, complaints or support calls. A feature can lift the first while quietly damaging the second. If the damage goes past the limit we agreed in advance, we pause or remove the feature, fix the cause and try again, because a gain in one place that costs more elsewhere is not a gain. We also check again after a few weeks, because early gains from novelty often fade.
